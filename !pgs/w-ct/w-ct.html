Wolfsbane & Hatred

Return to catalog

Hatred is one of the most difficult attributes that humans deal with philosophically. It exists, as does nearly everything, in a state of holiness and purity and righteousness and glory, but unlike most other extant things, it is difficult for people with the presence of the Triune God to assume in a Christ-like manner. Many people joy with the Spirit's joy; many people love with the Spirit's love; many people discern with the Spirit's discernment; very few hate with the Spirit's hatred. Hatred nearly always assumes itself as an accomplice the Accuser. We, being humans, often assume it anyway. The characters of Wolfsbane all assume it, in some form or fashion, and all of them feel justified for doing it, though some more than others. Hatred, however, belongs to the Lord as all things do; we are not called to feel righteous hatred as much as we are called to simply be righteous. [Psalm 101; Proverbs 6:16-19; 8:12-36; Amos 5; John 15; Titus 3] Human reproduction of the Lord's righteous hatred is wicked and destructive, as human reproductions of the godly always are; The Lord's wrath strengthens His creation, and humanity’s wrath destroys it. The Lord is able to lord over wrath; without Him, hatred is usually what controls people.

The most hateful person in this novel is its person of interest, Richard Gardiner. Gardiner has a troubled past. On a mission to see what kind of munitions the French rebellion forces might need to fight off the Germans in World War II, he was betrayed alongside a Frenchman by several in the British and American leadership in an attempt to stop communist forces in France from seizing the government, effectively replacing the government from prior to the German invasion and driving the Germans out of France, thereby winning the war for France. He tries to forget about the whole experience, but it sits inside him as a sleeper agent, reawoken by the careful placement of one of the men who helped attempt to kill Gardiner at a hotel Gardiner goes to on holiday nineteen years later. He is forced to kill that man in self defense, but this has removed all nineteen years of time between 1944 and 1963, and now Gardiner feels the need to exact his full revenge. After the incident, Gardiner returns to his hotel room, "went to the window and for a long time stood in silence. His skin became responsive to the external temperature rather than to the heat of thought, and the beach across the promenade was silver in the moonlight, A thin white moustache of foam showed where the tide was turning. Yes, he thought, again and again, it is like a cancer. It is eating me."[1] Gardiner is infested with a slurry of emotions, much more than any person could handle on their own. It turns him into someone else, molds him into that past self that he tried to shrug off. It completely overwhelms him. Latymer, a British intelligence member who remembers Gardiner from World War II, comes to try and talk Gardiner out of what he is doing fairly early on in the latter's mission and discovers in a car with him "that a younger man stared out at him. Gardiner was younger; Latymer had a presentiment that he had lost him, that his words already had a hollow ring. . . Latymer saw him as a man trying to sever bonds and codes he no longer believed or recognized. To kill Latymer. . . . The break would be sudden and complete."[2] Gardiner's hatred has completely consumed him. It has caused him to be someone else. Of course, the person Gardiner was before was already in great sin – he and his wife like trading affairs, he hates her and his kids, he has completely detatched himself from his family, et cetera. Gardiner was primed for this kind of breakdown by his lifestyle, his life.

Not all hatred is so blunt, however. Take one of Gardiner's betrayers, Constant, for example. His story is much like Gardiner's. Instead of handing him directly to death, NATO brushed him under the rug and considered him dead when he was captured by Soviet forces in a mission to find runaway internal German protection squadron members fleeing after their defeat in the war – just after World War II. A lot of the reason for this came from his role in World War II: "While he [Constant] was going up the snakes, he was always regarded as one. And when he went into the Russian zone, he was going down their ladder, the one they prepared for him. . . He had learned to irrevocably hate the people who had abandoned him in that tiny, damp, icy cell. . . He abandoned it [the British]. . ." He voices his motivation, that hatred, more clearly: "The years of the war, his sense of always being a despised implement of others, had crystallized in that filthy, stinking, tiny cell in Berlin when he knew his masters had abandoned him."[3] Therefore, Constant became a Soviet double agent for NATO, and has been for eighteen years. Constant, by directive of the Soviet internal forces who selected him for his suitable personality, does not voice his anger in brash and dramatic actions as Gardiner does, but Constant's response is just as outlandish: to infiltrate to the uppermost level of NATO, to conquer it for the Russians, to tear down the entire system that failed him, to feel something like powerful. His hatred is shown to be cool, but it burns as hot as Gardiner's and is influenced by the same kind of priming beforehand; like Gardiner, Constant's hatred becomes his sole being, his primary motivation for living, the end goal of his existence. It becomes what he ascribes to, what he worships. Consider also Constant's main rival within Britain, Aubrey. Aubrey's hatred is made clear right from the beginning of the novel, back in World War II: "Aubrey stared out the window, as if he had heard it all before – which was true. He did not trust his features to remain smooth as the man's affected patronage extended minute by minute, causing him to boil slowly. Constant was an old adversary."[4] This hatred is carried throughout Aubrey's life, throughout the entire novel. It inspires Aubrey to place one of Constant's closer underlings in a trap for Gardiner to kill. The trap being sprung is told from Latymer's point of view: "Fewer than fifty yards separated them [Gardiner and Constant's soldier]. Only feet of that completely open, the road and the frail white fence that would separate the two men for much too short a time. He had a sudden fear that Aubrey was enjoying the spectacle. He took the glasses from his eyes for a moment and saw Aubrey's features settled in grim satisfaction. . ."[5] Again, Aubrey's hatred of Constant takes over, influences all decision making, determines all actions taken. Nothing else is considered by Aubrey aside from this hatred once it takes full root at the end of this novel.

Then, not all hatred thinks of itself so negatively. Some sees itself as directly usurping the Lord in His position, as donning man's own righteousness and doing the right thing. Take Haussman. See, Gardiner's been set up. The man who tried to kill Gardiner was not there by accident; the man was placed there intentionally by a higher power – in fact, that higher power subtly influenced Gardiner to vacate to France to begin with. This power wanted Gardiner to kill all of those people, but especially his uppermost betrayers, "Wolf" and "Wolverine." This is worrying, but the novel reveals at the end that the set up is not quite as malevolent as the you are led to believe. "Wolf" and "Wolverine" are Constant and Van Lederer, the latter a very high ranking U.S. official, the latter having betrayed NATO to the Soviets, too. The French know that this is occurring and want the pair gone. The SDECE sees it as their responsibility to deal with pair; Haussman is their fieldsman. This group is the one influencing Gardiner, and helping him, on his mission. Haussman is fiery over the treachery to his country. Consider his thoughts on Etienne de Vaugrigard, one of Gardiner's betrayers and also one who helps Haussman with the setup: "Etienne knows about treason, he thought savagely. Twisted sense of loyalty, and Renaud goes to the Avenue Foch and Gardiner with him. Twisted again, and he supplies the OAS with money and guns to fight back at de Gaulle [the French President ousted by Germany in World War II], hero turned traitor. . ."[6] It is not too shocking that Haussman does allow Gardiner to kill Vaugrigard. Haussman later explains to Gardiner of the set up, when Latymer and Aubrey capture the latter and run into Haussman who then exposits his plans to the inattentive reader and to the British pair, "It was almost too wonderful, too convenient, to believe. I promised to protect him [Vaugrigard] against you, as long as you could be led to him. I must admit that when I checked on you, I assumed you had tried to make yourself forget the war and escaped into the skin of a civilized man, I brought Dupuy to you, a passive trigger for the kind of man you once were – I gambled that you had not changed too much."[7] Perhaps Haussman is doing "the right thing;" perhaps mercy will be bestowed upon the French, and this will turn out for the benefit of the West, and the consequences of their meddling reduced. Perhaps not. Haussman's plan is, as the other parties, maniacal. It skips over any attempt at diplomacy, although Latymer and Aubrey's conversations show that such diplomacy would truly be futile, as the other NATO nations hate the French for their own double-agent which was just removed; it skips over a jailing of the culprits on their own terms using the gathered evidence; and it skips over using special operatives no doubt much better than even Gardiner to assassinate the Soviet agents and goes straight to using a pawn just recently introduced and setting him on a killing spree that happens to align with the SDECE's motives by the end. It is spawned by a maniacal society, the postmodern West.

No one in this novel manages to portray righteous hatred. The only man who keeps himself pure is Latymer, who briefly hates his wife for falling so ill, but manages to move on in his work and with her death. Latymer's low will causes him to neglect what appears to be his duties to his country, but he is able to affect the completion of the SDECE's plans without the maniacal enthusiasms of Aubrey and Haussman and Gardiner, merely freeing Gardiner to do what is obvious to himself as the right thing: removing the Soviet threat to Europe. Gardiner, for what it is worth, seems to cool off somewhat as well for his final kill. After that though, is nothing. The novel ends right on the confirmation of Constant's death. In fact, in Craig Thomas's whole bibliography, Gardiner never appears again.[8] Do not act like you are not them. You could not have done better if you were in Gardiner's position, or Aubrey's or Latymer's or Constant's. Do not turn your face away from them. Do not act like you are not them. Job's friends felt the same way about Job, that they were safe and above all sin and harm merely because of knowing about God, when they were in fact far from achieving Job’s grace. Eugene Peterson commentates on Job 12, one of Job's responses to his friends:

"It's easy for the well-to-do to point their fingers in blame,
     for the well-fixed to pour scorn on the strugglers.
Crooks reside safely in high-security houses,
     insolent blasphemers live in luxury;
     they've bought a god who'll protect them. . .
Isn't it clear they all know and agree
     that GOD is sovereign, that he holds all things in his hand–
Every living soul, yes,
     every breathing creature?
Isn't this just all common sense,
     as common as the sense of taste?"[9]

Certainly no one in Wolfsbane is as righteous as Job, but we are to afford mercy and understanding to everyone – lest you think the Lord should not have been merciful and understanding with you? It is with humbleness of heart that you must approach those who you do not know and whose circumstances you do not understand, for you live now in a penthouse of wealth and sheltered living and cannot understand the sufferings of others, and therefore even if you can discern the truth you have no authority to comment or act. You would approach them hating them, with man's hatred. Only Christ has that kind of authority, and by separating yourself from him, you decrease yourself far below those who you wish to condemn. By letting your humanity reign, like Gardiner, you destroy yourself, and if you attempt to remove yourself from the situation, as Latymer, who had authority and wisdom to spare Constant's man well before Gardiner appeared, you destroy yourself. You can sit and watch all of the pieces move on your chessboard, but it is you who is being controlled also. Remember the higher spiritual powers that exist, ones that, like Haussman, would be more than willing to give you what you want if it helps them destroy the kingdom of Christ on Earth. Humanity's design only destroys, in hatred or in love, but where Christ's sword pierces, there is the most potent healing of all. You cannot avoid that blow forever. Christ will perfect your humanity, but you must know it is Christ-centered humanity you need if you are ever to approach. Christ will not force you; if you want an eternity of your sins, that is the place you will be sent to. Submit yourself to Christ, who is righteous to give salvation to all of the world.
