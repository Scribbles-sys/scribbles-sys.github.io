Death of a Hero & Impersonality

The Great War of 1914-1918 captures the imagination of many when trying to determine a point of failure or falling from the Lord's grace in the Western World, if not the World at large. Many seem to feel that this event triggered social malaise previously unknown, but this is far from true. The actuality of and the effects of the Great War have been overstated. The world of Christ's time was just as bad as ours was, in the same ways ours is. The Great War is a blister on a world that has long since been going through a slow and lethargic aging process that has been happening since Christ's renewal through His resurrection. History is watching the world drift further and further away from Christ, not cataclysmically but as a person drifts further and further into old age. The world is pulverized under the reign of Sin even in its most peaceful moments. Some are ripped apart unwillingly; most do the disintegrating themselves. This is easy to speak and difficult to see. Where does George Winterbourne, the subject of Death of a Hero, fall? Surely the world does things to him and effects him, but it would also seem there are many of those same things he takes on in sober conscious. Nonetheless, it cannot be denied that George is pushed into doing what he does – he is pushed towards the War as a consequence of being pushed away from everyone else. As society abandons The Lord, God of Abraham, it gleefully also abandons the personal nature that defines Him. [Zephaniah 3; Luke 15; John 11]

Why did George Winterbourne enlist in the first place? He was never drafted, and didn't have any need for it for his own survival because of his absent income or something similar. What pushed him into that war? The same thing that pushed the rest of the Western world into the war, at least by Aldington's view. In the beginning, when Winterbourne is first being shipped off. He sees some of the more hardened soldiers standing around and talking.

"He was disappointed that these men talked in such a trivial and uninteresting way. Hey felt they ought to be saying important things in Shakespearean blank verse. Something adequate to their experiences, to the intensity of manhood he instinctively felt in them and admired so humbly. But, of course, that was ridiculous of him. He felt that at once."[1]

The war didn't come from nowhere, certainly. A proper and concise analysis of this would be too much for this essay. Instead, we look to see where George's life had taken him; we look the see the trenches of pre-Great-War England and the distance it fought to evoke. This distance is concisely explained with this observation by Winterbourne. He had somehow painted a picture of reality representative enough to allow him to know truth and genuineness when it is presented to him and yet be confounded with its image when he actually sees it with his own eyes. This is because, all of his life, George had been pushed away of all of those who know him. There is first George's father, George Augustus. George Augustus's mother lords over him and directs his life to the greatest of her ability, while his father was more or less absent. As Aldington describes it, "Dear Mama had her talons deep into George Augustus, vamped on him hideously; and was content. Dear Papa prayed in the garden and read the Right Hnble. etcetera's novels, and was uneasily content. George himself was pretty comfortable. . ."[2] This is important, for, as Aldington mentioned just before this, "Still, George Augustus was pretty comfortable. The one thing he wanted in life was to be pretty comfortable."[3] This line alone shows the issue between all three. George's mother steals the resources of those around her for comfort. George's father ignores those around him for the same reason. George takes on the features of and caters to both for the same reason. Comfort is antithetical for closeness and connection. A friendship demands times of doing that which you most want not to do, for example times of babysitting and listening to wailing after a murdered friendship and being unable to help and times of spending hours trying to troubleshoot broken septic systems and all manners of other things. This is true for the world at large, though – a comfortable world will not stick its face in the dirt to admire the insects that travel about – it hates the insects anyway – or spend hours waiting for a bird to show, spend weeks worth of time in raw hours learning their behavior patterns, or soak its clothes beyond any ability to naturally dry to feel the lapping of lake water. It is this very kind of personal closeness that the world has hated. Such a world certainly hates Christ, He who has no home, He who calls all of the world into great suffering so that they may draw nearer to Him. These preliminary things would turn the world away, but the world hates Him for without Him there is no true closeness. To draw nearer to Him is to be closer to everything, for the world is His Creation. This issue counterintuitively involves sex. Sex is one way in which humans experience deep and personal connection, and for this it must be destroyed, but at the same time sex is one of two things, the other being food, completely necessary for human existence and the makeup of unbridled basic pleasure, and as such is exploited to no end by the human mind. This predicament is a main factor in Victorian society shaping the way it did, and this ignorance causes George Augustus and the woman he eventually courts by flaunting his parents&apod; money, Isabel Hartley, to have one bad experience together that gives them George Winterbourne. George Augustus adopts the distance of his father, and Isabel adopts the deep dependency on her son for satisfaction in life that George Augustus's mother had. There is much, much fighting in the Winterbourne house, mainly between Isabel and George Augustus's mother, the family drops into comparative poverty, and eventually George's mother is so upset at George for taking a career in journalism that George Augustus kicks him out of the house.

With his family having pushed him away, George is forced to look for something to connect to in London. London is the center for new talk in England, the capital of progression, of breaking the chains of the society they were born into and forging a new society. Unfortunately, this new society requires the same chains the old one had. The best example of this is George's new lover turned wife, Elizabeth. They meet each other at a party for artists in London and hit it off after realizing they are both progressive fauxrevolutionaries. This doesn't last long; concisely, Fanny and Reggie enter their lives, and they fall in love with their other in the pair and enjoy the other's company more than each other's. Then Elizabeth fears she's become pregnant and demands George marry her and become a "proper couple". . . until she goes to a doctor after the marriage and finds out she can't be pregnant and is merely sick. As the author puts the aftermath,

"When Elizabeth reoccupied the Sexual Freedom line, without knowing it she did so for herself alone, and not for George. If George liked to accept the subsequent Elizabeth-Reggie affair. . . all well and good. . . But when it came to Elizabeth's accepting of the Fanny-George affair in the same spirit, that was a very different matter. Elizabeth now felt somehow responsible for George, and feeling responsible translated itself into keeping possession of..."[4]

This miserable outcome prompts even the narrator of the novel to say "They had become a 'family,' the eternal triangle of father, mother, child, which is so much more difficult and disagreeable and hard to deal with, and so much more productive of misery, than the other triangle of husband, wife, lover"[5] this about George Augustus and Isabel, and similar things about marriage throughout the novel. There is some truth to this, as there is some truth to the horrors ascribed to the Great War. The truth is more complicated than that, of course, as this book illustrates; the way of life George and Elizabeth sought was different from their parents, perhaps, but they were the same people their parents were, which made any pretense of change meaningless. The author is similarly sympathetic to George, rightly so: "You see, they cannot really kill the spark [that George has] if it is there, not with all their bullyings and codes and prejudices and thorough manliness. For, of course. They are not manly at all, they are merely puppets, the products of the system – if it may be dignified by that word."[6] This is true, of course, but nonetheless they all still bully and prejudice George, Elizabeth and Fanny and the elites of London and everyone else, and George eagerly takes on this distance. This spark is the sole solace in a world where nothing else is even vaguely correctly aligned, including the spark's beholder. They have pushed away anything that even seems like Christ, because Christ is the worst thing to them. Any risk of salvation is a risk not worth taking. As Shaw writes about the Great War, "At all events it is clear that the kingdom of the Prince of Peace has not yet become the kingdom of this world. His attempts at invasion have been resisted far more fiercely than the Kaiser's"[7] George's parents failed him, his ancestors failed him, his wife failed him, and most importantly he failed himself. He boarded the Titantic along with anyone else, and had perhaps even less reservations about it than they, seeing how more accepting of Elizabeth's affairs he was. What little good George has in him is smothered collectively by the whole world. They push him away, and eventually the dreadful War so obvious, the War even sought after, demanded by some of the populace, inevitably hits, and George's last hope dies; he falls into oblivion.

George Winterbourne enlists for the Great War in 1916. He has nothing with him nor for him anymore in England. He trains, and he is shipped off to the docks to make way towards France. He does not die instantly, of course. War is not one big, large battle that rages for years. "People at a distance thought of the fighting as heroic and exciting, in terms of cheering bayonet charges or little knots of determined men holding out to the last Lewis gun. That is rather like counting life by its champagne dinners and forgetting all the rest."[8] War is mostly shivering with pneumonia while trying to dig a trench for George. He isn't even able to tell there are people on the other side of their field for the most part. The other trench buddies don't make good company, for they're all in the same position; they don't make bad company, neither, especially Major Thorpe Evans. George views Evans's tastes as abhorrent, as ignorant and inhibited, as a cause of societal failing even, and yet he counts Evans a trusted comrade one can hold on to. They grow close as Evans selects George to be his runner. It is the only semblance of closeness George feels, the only taste of those soldiers he witnessed when shipping out, and it is continually threatened by the stress that has plagued Evans and that has infected Winterbourne. The disgust of being sick, of having diarrhea all of the time, of not being able to stop from messing yourself from the lack of places to relieve yourself, makes George feel less than human. The shelling is constant, and sometimes it becomes very fierce and destructive, but actual Germans are rarely seen. George is sent a letter by Fanny, and she mentions that Reggie is experimenting with deadly gas on monkeys for the war effort and he vomits. George and his fellow soldiers are hiding in a cellar, about to go up and fight the Germans for the first time, when "A rat ran down the cellar stairs and began to nibble something. The beast was exactly level with Winterbourne's head. He shoved a cartridge into the breech of his rifle, murmuring, 'Why should a dog, a horse, a rat have life, and they no breath at all?' He aimed very carefully and pulled the trigger; there was a terrific bang in the confined cellar, and the rat was smashed dead in the air."[9] When they do go up and meet their first Germans, they're already prisoners of war and being sent back behind the front. Throughout all of this, George avoids multiple attempts to be returned home from the front lines, saying something to the tune of "I had and still have a feeling I ought to to spend the War in the ranks and in the line."[10] The only time he returns home is when he needs additional training to become an officer, and Elizabeth and Fanny have both moved on from him, Fanny naturally finding someone else to tempt and Elizabeth growing even more attached to her other lover in George's absence. There is nothing left for George. He cannot go back, physically speaking. Returning to the front an officer is much more stressful, as George feels as though he has to fight his own men to keep them all from killing themselves. The horrors of the war grow stronger with the passing of time, as more of it accumulates. "He moved through impressions like a man hallucinated. And every incident seemed to beat on his brain Death, Death, Death. All the decay and deaths of battlefields entered his blood and seemed to poison him." For example, "He ordered a new latrine to be dug out from the trench, and thrice the digging had to be abandoned because they came upon terrible black masses of decomposing bodies."[11]

In the only real combat we see George Winterbourne in, he dies. Being pushed away by his family, his industry, his friends, and his attempts at lovers, George finds himself pushed all the way into violent oblivion. George is, in a sense, a microcosm of the entire world, so obsessed with being absent of everything that it creates a bloody nightmare that existed all along but showed itself in great short bursts. That spark, the joy he kept alive in him; his manliness, his humanity, is precious, important, to be lauded, but it does not put him above the "common man". When George sees those simple men come home from the war, when he sees how great they are, he realizes that he, too, is "common man;" everyone is a "common man," and no kinds nor depth of interests nor talents nor specialties will elevate one above the rest. Not that Winterbourne does away with his intelligentsia, though it does start to slip from him as the stresses of the Great War build on his person. George's issue, society at large's issue, is its obsession with the systems it works in as opposed to the people and reasoning for its genesis. This is also why Elizabeth acts in all of the ways she does, why everyone does what they do. Why doesn't Christ act? Why doesn't He send someone to speak to these people? Why doesn't he rouse their hearts in some supernatural way? Why doe he allow the world to descend in the way that it has? The answer to these questions is simply that you need to mind your own business. [Isaiah 40:9-31; Job 38-41, 42:1-6; Romans 11] Christ is personable even through, and perhaps due to, this incomprehensive nature. The world seeking to push us down and separate us from Life itself should prompt us to seek out the Lord who gave that life to us, and the despair of living must make us crawl as close to Christ as we can manage. We will be totally lost without these things. We will be dead without the Holy Spirit, dead in a way a mass of rotten corpses could not obtain. We need communion and we need community. The Lord is gracious enough to give us Himself and His Body, the Church. May we take these gifts with thanksgiving, and may we know that the Lord is faithful to His promises far beyond our own faithfulness.
