Saint Dale & Veneration

It would be silly to suggest that Christ would have us live in a world of His creation and then not expect us to see the marksmanship of His Creation within it. There are people in this world worth glorifying: a great old friend, a church member who is always there for their congregation, one who makes breakthrough in the sciences, a talented artist, et cetera. Just as one can behold the Creation such that they forget its creator, one too can focus so much on a person that they should dethrone the Holy Spirit within themselves and replace them with this person. It is precisely at this point that veneration dismays the Lord and at no other point. This is the focus of St. Dale: people who are crazy about famed NASCAR driver Dale Earnhardt, perhaps the most famous and the most beloved of those drivers, who died in a crash on the track on February 18, 2001. A group of people sign up for a tour to honor his legacy and the greater legacy of NASCAR. The people who embark on this pilgrimage will end up tying together with one another and fixing some of each other's problems, and as they move along we are given a tour of the sport and of the perception of it. We see how highly everyone esteems Earnhardt Sr. and NASCAR and how much they mean, while also seeing the folly of self indulgence in these things.

The most obvious indication of this manner of obsession is the creation of, either physically or metaphorically, of shrines. The first of these the Number Three Pilgrims, named after Earnhardt Sr.'s racing number, stop at is Richard Petty's museum. It is described as "if it had been designed by a firm of architects who specialized in branch banks;" one character says "It looks so dignified."[1] The museum itself contains the race cars the Petty family have driven over the years, as well as belt buckles and dolls belonging to the family in the back of the museum. This museum is designed to be homely and approachable; not merely accessible to those not heavily invested in the sport, though it is also this, but intentionally taking grandeur, or the feeling of it, and delivering it unto the visitors. Just after the Petty Museum is the DEI memorial to Earnhardt Sr., "shrine to the man himself,"[2] which is much more grand than the Petty Museum:

"An hour later, and a few miles down the road, Shane McKee stood in a dimly lit entry foyer, beside the roped-off black number 3 car. He gazed down the dark and cavernous hallway, illuminated only by the faint glow of pictire lights above each exhibit, and then he began to walk away from the sunshine of the entryway, and down the long dark hall of memories.
"The place looked more like church than church, he thought."[3]

An Episcopalian clergyman on the trip, Bill Knight, echoes the sentiment, calling the place "Opus DEI." It is a place of rememberance too, but it is different from the Petty Museum in that it beckons the visitor to look up on the grandeur of its subject instead of placing it within them; it caters to their yearning to draw nearer to a saint who has been elevated by the people. Finally, and most notable to the clergyman, is Talledega Texaco Walk of Fame, a memorial to Davey Allison, who died in a helicopter crash in Talledega, and other famous NASCAR drivers. It is a garden of young hardwood trees, with a brick path through the middle and an oval path, mimicking a NASCAR course, surrounding the entire area. People can buy bricks and leave messages behind upon the brick path. It is most unlike the two museums because it is outside and peaceful. The focus is less on the sport; though there is a wall dedicated to Allison and plaques to other drivers, there is intention put upon the visitor and not upon the sport or the drivers, as the visitor is not guided or put upon a set path, and being outside lends the visitors of the park to drift their thoughts elsewhere – even being denominated as a park indicates its wide range of uses. Knight takes particular liking to this park because it reminds him of the shrines medieval pilgrims liked to visit. He tells another pilgrim, "Yes. Something beautiful that came about because of a tragedy."[4] These places all are not mere biographies, but are, in St Dale, stops sought out by wayward pilgrims. They are shrines focused on one person, but looking beyond them, looking at how they lived and what they lived for as much as the person who lived or is living. Most obvious about all of these places is how severe and solemn they are – they are not, as the Wall Street firm man Terence had thought before seeing how dignified they are, hillbilly showoff barns, but are sites where one can come and respect the sport and the participants in the sport, respect both the existence of it and what its existence means to the pilgrim. In that respect, they are also retrospective. Though the Petty Museum, for example, overall shows the direction in which that NASCAR family is going, it does this by tracing out where the family has been. It focuses less on, say, how the family dresses or passes the time now, but rather shows previously used belt buckles and dolls as mementos of the life they had lived before the current moment. This makes apparent that NASCAR is serious. It is more than just a past time; as Kyle Petty said, "NASCAR racing is not a sport as much as it is a lifestyle – among competitors but also among fans."[5] It's beyond a look or a brand but a defining part of their personality, much of the same way that many people identify themselves entirely with certain intellectual properties or entertainment studios, but much deeper and less caustic than those examples because they have more tangible effects on their everyday lives and their attitudes and behaviors, values, mores, et cetera, and are representitave of tangible aspects of themselves and their lives, perhaps even made to be shrines towards those things by its fans. The events, history, and cultural influence of NASCAR defines many people. It is their center, their focus. Their misplacement is not necessarily in their care for it, but in how ravenous they are towards NASCAR and Dale Earnhardt Sr. in their supposed care. It is all-consuming. They worship Dale and his lifestyle, and sing their praises to him. They do not merely consider his ways in the grand scheme of things, but frame him so that he casts a shadow over all things; they do not merely find good in his ways, but seek to emulate him as accurately and entirely as possible; they do not merely consider his view of life, but skew their attitudes about his own, orbit his thoughts as close as they can, and excessively mimic or impose upon him viewpoints and attitudes; they do not merely acknowledge their similarities, but imagine deep and tangible connections to him as a person, connections which are beneficial if not outright salvific.

There is in this last point the severe apophenia that the characters express, mostly Shane. Shane seems to have the strongest attachment to Dale among the Pilgrims. He is shown to be the most devastated at the loss, and he takes it the hardest, and the most immaturely. His demeanor changes, though, when he hears news of a goat:

"'Listen, Karen, I just heard about this, and you won't believe it: a goat was born in Florida [the state of Dale's last race].'
"'It happens,' I [Karen] said.
"'Yes, but listen to this: the goat has a number three in white hair on its side! A number three.'"

As Karen says after explaining this, "that's what set him off."[6] The whole reason Shane is with the Pilgrims is on account of this goat, so he can see it in person once the pilgrimage is over, and decides to tack a marriage in Bristol Motor Speedway before the race that track having onto the beginning of the tour. They are far from the only ones being married there on that particular day. Shane's tendency to see connections only grew from the goat forward. He seeks out some sign to make it clear to him that Earnhardt Sr. and those connected to the racer are divinely inspired, divine within themselves, chosen ones of stock car racing. He relays to the preacher:

"Old Number 3 finally broke the jinx. That was February 15, 1998. That's important. That date. . .
"He died . . . February 18, 2001. . . that is exactly three years and three days after he won the race.
"Since he died, he hasn't lost the Daytona 500. . .
"No, he can. He has an owner. . . he formed his own company. . ."If one of the DEI drivers wins again next February that'll be three wins in a row for DEI, ending in the year two thousand and three. You see what I'm talking about? . . .
"Don't you see? Death transformed him. He reached a higher power."[7]

He goes on to compare this to Star Wars and Lord of the Rings, as opposed to the transfiguration of Christ as Knight fears. Shane yearns for Dale to mean something important and transcendent, to be a higher power he can look up to, to believe that Dale's death means something. He feels robbed of meaning in his life and tries to impose it in ways it cannot possibly exist. He lost something important to him and seeks to hold on to it however he can, including lying and making things up. The Pilgrims eventually confront him about his error once they finally all hear of it:

"Karen was shaking her head. 'You made it through the first couple of laps, I think, Shane. Until they showed Kevin Harvick's car up close, anyhow. The Goodwrench logo. . . and later you made me tell you who won.'
"'That's right. And you said [Dale Earnhardt] Junior won it.'
"'I know, Shane,' she murmured. 'I know I said it. It just wasn't true, that's all. . . I'll bet half a dozen times since then people have mentioned that Ward Burton won. We even saw a program on television where they said it, but it seemed like it just went right over your head.'"[8]

This reverence of Dale is so strong in Shane's heart that he is unable to see reality. It is not that Shane hides from it, but rather his heart is so stalwart against the true nature of things that its assails against him are not felt by his dead body. His members are entirely dedicated to his cause, DEI, and anything else withers and decays within his soul. No one in this novel approaches Shane's hyperbole, but their type does not differ from his. They are here because they harbor an improper reverence for man and material. It is, as mentioned, beyond acknowledgment, beyond admiration, and beyond veneration, all things proper for the good in Christ's creation; it is reverence. They take that which we look upon fondly and imbue it with divine properties, declaring it God and serving it. Beyond this, they seek to establish themselves with Dale's elevation. Justine identifies with his aggression, Shane views him as a center for life, Terence views him as an ultimate connector in a life that seems absent of all connection, Bekasu sees him as liberation from her caged political life, et cetera. Dale is a holy figure, and naturally these characters place themselves on his side, step up to his holiness just as they are placing the driver there, convincing themselves that Earnhardt Sr. would lift them up with him. All of NASCAR has some stake in this camaraderie; the driver of the car, as is also expounded in this books, is almost always dependent on their team to tell them, through the driver's headset, about conditions on the track and if some special rule has been called or if a wreck happened, et cetera. This extends onto the fans of the sport, who put themselves just behind these teammates as well, feeling as though they are both spectators cheering on their driver. This feeling of connection tends to be fabricated and exaggerated, even if the community around it is not. Shane is a hyperbolic but not unrealistic portrayal of this. Do not look over the good that had as arisen within this, however, which is the Pilgrims turning to each other as they grow familiar with one another. Everyone finds connection within each other, a connection that is facilitated by their love of Dale. Sarah, accompanying Terence, calls in a favor from her ex husband, as the latter works at a mechanic school; if Shane can obtain a degree there, he can become a mechanic for NASCAR. This arrangement allows him and Karen to honeymoon proper in Florida; Matthew, a kid that Knight priest is watching over is relieved of his loneliness as Justine elects to take care of him; Bekasu is able to realize what she is missing within the Pilgrim's devotion; et cetera. Shane, at the beginning of the novel, is mentioned to have started an initiative for his church, "Driving for Dale," which drives church members who have no means of transporting themselves to the grocery store and doctor's visits, et cetetra. There are many other characters not mentioned here, who all come together at the death of one of them from Alzheimer's at the end of the pilgrimage. This is not facilitated by Dale, as perhaps some of these characters believe, but they are able to find good in their common ground, and common ground in their good.

Consider the Nehushtan. [Numbers 21; 2 Kings 18] The Nehushtan is only mentioned briefly throughout the entirety of the Bible. When the Hebrews are wandering, and they cry against the Lord and His deliverance from the kings of the region, the snakes of the area turn against them. As opposed to removing the snakes or striking them down, as the people requested Moses to pray for, the Lord orders a statue of the serpents created that the Hebrews may look upon it and live. Jesus refers to this incident when speaking to Nicodemus, perhaps because he would be aware of a relatively obscure passage such as this, to refer to His own coming death and the reason for it, the thing which the bronze serpent always typefied. This statue does not stay in the wilderness but is taken by the Hebrews and eventually becomes an idol, treated as a deity. It stays this way until Hezekiah has it destroyed, deeming it beyond salvation and mockingly calling it nehushtan, a piece of brass. The trail to follow, then, is that the serpent was created so that the Hebrews may have some way of looking upon the Lord and being reminded of both their own fault in their sins and their salvation from them, and the Hebrews' view of the serpent eventually degraded such that the serpent could no longer remind them of the Lord, but had become an object of worship itself; the serpent had pointed to something greater, but the Hebrews ceased to look where it was pointing. This is itself the definition of a secular shrine, that which points nowhere and exists only in itself. We must remember that we do not live in an existing world but in a created world. This must not lend itself into a hatred for that created world. Veneration does have its place. We are called to care for our world, to respect it. We naturally find beauty in it. [Psalm 8] Everywhere in Scripture that the beauty of nature is mentioned, it is always because the Lord is within it, because the trees and the birds and the rocks all praise the Lord and know of His Wisdom. It is for these reason that we lift it up, that we study and catalog the birds, where they came from and what will become of them, why we rise up in our chest when a dear friend visits, why hearing of the great deeds both of neighbors in our time and those from long ago who we remember brings tears to the eyes, et cetera. We venerate these things because they are the good works of Christ and His good work deserves recognition for what it is, both in and towards itself and towards its Creator. So while the characters in this novel quite obviously faun over the materials of their world, they understand the worth inherent within them, know that there are good things in life that we ought to hold up, that people who represent us in higher courts are worth admiring, that people who stand as we know we should stand should be praised for this. To neglect this too is to turn our face away from Christ. We have done an awful job caring for the Lord's world and must repent. Nor must any of this lead us to forget that which the statue had originally meant, the longwithstanding lovingkindness of the Lord and His salvation toward us, for to do this is to join in those who worshiped the Nehushtan, to fixate our eyes upon our world only and deny them any view of our Lord.
