Big City Dreams & Obligation

Return to catalog

What is a follower of Christ supposed to do? Neither the Gospel nor the Law are simple regulations on how to live; following Christ is not simply doing this one thing and not doing that other thing. Salvation is commitment to the Messiah of all the Earth, a submission to the Lord of Abraham and Isaac and Israel. This is the obligation, and it is an obligation. The man who sits back and does nothing is no follower; Christ has gone elsewhere and has left you behind. We, at our best, stay behind. At our worst, we are going somewhere else. Many came before Christ and presented what they felt were their obligations. [Matthew 8:18-23; Luke 9:57-62] They were, in fact, their obligations, and that was the issue Christ had – doing the will of God on Earth should be your sole obligation. These people at best did not trust the Lord to enact His Will in their lives and at worst actively denounced the Lord and chased that which was aside from Christ. Perhaps the one truly worse thing about our lives compared to those men in Scripture is that there is now so many other things to chase after; some of us do well to be focused on the asides the men from Scripture were wasted by. We often focus on constructing alternate personas or building imaginary kingdoms. In Big City Dreams, we follow two kids finding a life for themselves in Night City. Both feel they are required something that not only is not their obligation but is entirely impossible for them to perform on their own.

The first of this pair is Tasha. Our introduction to the character is Tasha driving like a hooligan trying to escape the cops and running over some guy quitting his job. Tasha and Mirek have stolen a bunch of corpses because they still have augmentation implants and they want to sell them to a ripperdoc. This is Tasha's and Mirek's life – doing illegal odd-jobs to try and make money. Tasha loves doing this – in fact, Tasha's main goal in life seems to be improving the pair's standing in the "Night City underground," always living a fast life. You're not somebody unless you’re living that fast, underground life, so Tasha does their best to build their street credit. They don't take names, they don't think twice; they're a real hustler. Being a hustler is all Tasha sees to being a human being. When Mirek is trying to think about his life, Tasha hasn't the patience for that.

"Can't see the stars from the city. Look. No stars."
"Huh?! Come on! No time for bulls–!"[1]

Mirek wants nothing to do with the hustler life and is only trying to make enough money to survive without selling his soul to corporations or others. Tasha, once Mirek voices the first sign of resistance to Tasha's love of the game, blows their top at Mirek: "Too much? Too much, motherf–? Night city is a f– highway! Can't do forty when everyone's doin' eighty! You're lucky you're ridin' shotgun in my hotrod, baby! Or you'd end up a f– bloody smear on the pavement!"[2] There is a sense for an obligation for speed – in Night City, you absolutely have to go hard or else you die. If you don't die a legend, then what's the point? Of course, Tasha has no idea how Night City really works; they come up with the idea, after overhearing a conversation with that ripperdoc, to kill a girl from the Valentinos, one of many gangs, who plays around with and breaks the heart of an Animal girl, another gang. Tasha expects to receive a bounty for avenging the poor girl's heart. Instead, the Animals say "I ain't playin' your kiddie games!"[3] and try to kill the pair. Neither gang cared all that much about the issue as a gang per se, but Freese's murder is enough to incite a gang war. Tasha's meddling has made the situation in the city worse. Therefore, the cohorts who pulled the stunt have to die, only for the chance to evade war. Tasha does die, of course, not a legend but a bloody smear on the pavement. Tasha dies touting a god that will not save them, the god of personhood. They believe being "somebody," leaving a mark, being productive, influencing the world, having a presence, is enough. Enough for what? None of it amounted to anything tangible. They believe being "somebody" is something they are oblidged to do. Tasha was, in reality, blind to the way that the world works, consumed by their own needs instead. They tried to construct a personage in lieu of being a real human being.

Do not think Mirek is without vice. Whereas Tasha seeks pleasure from nonsensical punkish behavior, Mirek seeks pleasure from complacency. Where Tasha seeks personhood in chaotic control, Mirek seeks it in pure fantasy. Tasha introduces Mirek to a violent braindance – a "braindance" being like a movie, but you are put in the position of the actor through neurotransmitters so you see and feel just like the actor does – and when Mirek doesn't take well to it, the braindance vendor offers something else: a series about a family growing up on a farm outside of Night City. Mirek is enthralled by this vision. Without any home, this home becomes extremely tantalizing. Mirek seeks these braindances as a form of escape from facsimile in his real life:

"Tasha's out partyin'…"
"What, you're not in the mood for revelry?"
"Not so much."
"So who will you become today? What kinda crazy life will you live?"
"Do ya… uh… got more of that family I seen last time?"[4]

This seeking plays out in Mirek's real life, as he grows obsessed with this series and wants to locate the farm it is filmed on. He has no home with Tasha, and there is no other place to carve out in Night City; the city will merely chew him up and spit him out if he tries that route. The alternatives to edgerunning are enslavement by corporations or wandering aimlessly and homeless. So, Mirek tries to build himself a separate home, using the farm in this braindance as a basis. This fantasizing does turn out to come with one tangible benefit: when Mirek barely escapes the shootout, the only place safe he can think of to go is that farm outside of Night City. The braindance vendor is able to scrounge the address for it. Mirek bolts there immediately. The old man of the family is the only one left. Not even any of the cornstalks remain in the desolate fields; the house is worn down, and the silo has fallen. The wife and daughter left that life for Night City, unsatisfied with the life they had, downright miserable in it, feeling stuck in an unproductive cycle and unable to fight the temptation to have more. On the last page, the old man who recorded the braindances says "Can't pull a U-turn on the highway, ain't that right, son? They'd never come back. Some folks can't ever settle down. Never satisfied with what they had before. Me? I got everythin' I need right here."[5] The old man has nothing left except his place. He, similarly, would be demolished by Night City; trying to run would not fix anything. Mirek cannot see this; he feels obligated to avoid the malice and suffering of the world, but the destroyed and abandoned reality of the farm from the braindances shows that there is no such escape from such suffering on this Earth. The farm is not his home. There is no home Mirek can build, for he is not powerful enough to establish such a monumental kind of place. The only thing you will be left with if you put your trust in an earthly home is disruption and destruction.

The tragedy in looking at this in our own lives is that we too have no more home. The world has desolated our sense of self to the point that we now view life only as competition, friends only by their return on investment; actions, decisions, beliefs are all determined at random and at will. We are destroyed. We barely have functional shelters, let alone time spent with our kin, being there with them in silent empty moments, watching after their children or nieces and nephews when we visit, coming together for the community one experiences in eating. We have been rid of it all for endless noise, childless freedom, and quick and easy eating, and we could not be more joyous about it. Why even bother staying, then? Why should the Christian stay in this wretched place when that longing for the soul seems so close to us? Because we are obligated to. [Numbers 14; Micah 6; Romans 14; 2 Corinthians 12; Philippians 1] It should be fairly obvious; isn't everything you do because you are obligated to do it for the Lord? Do you not recognize Christ's callings for your life as that which is love? Do you not see that the Holy Spirit is more than willing and able to lead you towards fulfillment, towards full humanity? Do you not know that your design and fulfillment hinges entirely on Christ and that your current state of being is determined entirely by and through Him? Your obligation, in short, is to deny all of the things of this world, ensuring that you begin with yourself, and accept all of the things of the Kingdom of Heaven. Do not think you can be your own person, for Christ created us so that He is our person; do not think you can build you a home on this Earth, for the Kingdom of Heaven is our home. Christ is the only one who can fulfill the obligations set out for us, so let all of us turn to Him and give our lives over to Him.
