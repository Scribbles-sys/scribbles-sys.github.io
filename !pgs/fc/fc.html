Frenchman's Creek & Misalignment

Return to catalog

Frenchman's Creek begins with a very bitter Dona St. Columb: "And all because of an impulse blindly obeyed, a sudden boiling up of resentment against the futility of her life. . . This sense of futility had been growing upon her for many months, nagging at her now and again for many months like dormant toothache. . ."[1] She is out to Cornwall, to Navron, for at the bare minimum a break from a husband she hates and a London she despises, a life she dreads and a love she hates; in short, she is fed up being a wife, mother, and courtlady. She finds, however, that her house in Cornwall, long unused by her, has been stealthily taken over by a French pirate, Jean-Benoit Aubrey. When the two meet, Dona finds Jean-Benoit drawing sketches of sea birds, and as time goes on she confirms his gentleness and intelligence. In the pirate, Dona finds a possible route out of her predicament – giving up the feminine roles she has in London, and taking on the masculine role of cabin boy. While there are more obvious and immediate issues with the solution presented, Dona ultimately finds that, beyond any moral qualms, the solution doesn't really work. It not only does not provide her with real escape from her roles and who she presently is, but it doesn't directly tackle the issue with Dona's disillusionment, mostly because Dona herself is to limited in her framing of it. The identification and realization of her problem, the pursuit of reprieve from her problem, and the folly in the chosen solution to her problem is the overarching structure of the novel, and it too represents the folly generally of seeking manmade solutions to manmade problems, as opposed to turning to Christ in the midst of our problems and submitting to His plan for our lives and for His Bride the Church as a whole.

Dona's bitterness and disillusion is general at the beginning of the novel, but once Jean-Benoit becomes a tangible person, the general disillusion becomes a more defined displeasure with her feminine roles in life. She first expresses this when fishing:

"She frowned, concentrating on her task, and because the worm wriggled she jabbed her fingers with the hook. She swore under her breath, and glancing up, saw that he was laughing at her.
"'I cannot do it,' she said, angrily, 'why must a woman be so useless at these things?'"[2]

It is a macrocosm of her larger complaints: she feels useless, she feels her life is meaningless, she is bored with the way things are and has no physical way of breaking out of them. Even at the Navron house, while she feels more at peace, she feels she has yet to escape, for her children and her servants from her London life are still there with her, as much as she loves them. Meeting the pirate, however, completes this picture for her. Initially, the Jean-Benoit’s reaction to this is "It is a pity indeed that you are not a boy, you could have come with me," but to this the mistress replies, "Why must I be a boy to do that?"[3] Jean-Benoit gives many reasons, but Dona stays stalwart in her want to be a part of the La Mouette. She then adopts fully this solution to her problem: if being a mother, a wife, a woman, is so draining and dreadful, if the life of London with her husband is so sickening and crushes her under the weight of itself, if she cannot bear to be Dona anymore, than she must become a sailor, a pirate, a roughneck, a man, and be free on the seas, free to go anywhere and be anything as men are free to do those things. It shows itself as the particular way out of her predicament moreso once she is actually on the ship: "Even the day was infectious, and the hot sun, the fresh westerly breeze, the blue water, and Dona had a ridiculous longing to be a man amongst them, to handle ropes and blocks, to climb aloft the tall raking spars and trim the sails. . ."[4] This fondness for the men is central to her escape, for she envies the littlest of those men before her, and sees their lives as freedom and joy in comparison to her own failures. Seeing this in the crew and herself, she adopts the identity completely, having found it preferable, as she says to Jean-Bneoit: "She is a cabin-boy, the most insignificant member of your crew."[5] Upon adopting this, she finds that she can, in fact, sail a ship, she can be its helmsman and keep it on the wind. She is committed to this role now and decides to come ashore with Jean-Benoit and others to help more directly in piracy. Later on in their mission to steal a merchant vessel, Dona dresses up as a boy, to try and lure the vessel's owner out of their house and cause commotion:

"Dona trailed her hand a moment in the water, which was warm, with a velvet softness about it, the phosphorescence gleaming like a shower of stars, and she thought, smiling to herself in the darkness, that at last she was playing the part of a boy, which as a child she had so often longed to be, watching her brothers ride off with her father, and she gazing after them with resentful eyes, a doll thrown aside on the floor in disgust."[6]

Therefore, as mentioned, Dona leaves her life at Navron behind and joins the crew of the La Mouette. She joins them on an expedition to capture a merchant vessel owned by one of the residents of Cornwall, and while she does not herself directly assist in capturing the vessel, she is, so to speak, boots-on-the-ground in pirating a little boat of her own with a crewmate and disturbing and distracting the local population, after which she reconnects with the new ship. She then, with the help of another of Jean-Benoit's crewmates, slips him weapons in jail once he is actually caught, and helps him disappear back to his boat. There is something truly freeing about this excursion. In London, all she could do in this regard is play pranks on people in the aristocracy, which only made her feel embarrassed and confirmed her feelings of uselessness, made her feel she wasn't doing anything more than clawing at a cage. Being a pirate is actually doing something, though – she actually steals a ship from, as she contextualizes it, the hands of the dull and isolated to be brought into the hands of those that need it, she actually breaks someone she loves out of jail. She is not a failure, she has "made a success in life,"[7] as she felt she had been robbed of. She has affected the world around her and the world within her. Therefore, being a pirate allows her to be connected with other people – she is doing real things with real people, instead of the mere barhopping and absent housekeeping of her life in London. Her life in London has her with a lot of people, of course, especially her husband and children, but she isn’t connected with anyone. Her husband is aloof and distant all of the time, seeing her only as an object he can dote; her servants are simply there to do their job and, before William and his secrets, the mistress never has any relationship with them; Harry has friends, one friend who even lustfully eyes her, but she cannot connect with any of them by their proxy with Harry; the court, the aristocracy, so on and so forth, are so distant from her as to not even be worth mentioning their names. Dona has no one to reach out to. She has her children, and they hold her together and give her something to focus the love and spirit she has within her on, hence why she encumbers herself with them on her vacation, but little kids cannot prove good company. So, this pirate's life not only gives her one particular man who she can truly understand and who truly understands her, but gives her a community that she can partake in meaningfully. See again the quote of longing to be with the men in manning the ship, how Dona longs to be a part of their work, to be not merely manning a ship but manning a ship "amongst them," with them, bonded with them in their act of sailing the ship, feeling them through her work and vice versa. Of course, not only does La Mouette give Dona a general community but a particular person to latch on to, a person who understands who she is and how she feels, a person who is willing to take her actuality and not just acknowledge and talk about it but admire it, which is something else no one in her family or in London at large affords her. There is only one brief moment where Harry approaches being considerate and understanding of Dona. "I don't know what I think. I only know I'd give everything in the world to make you happy, but the cursed trouble is I don't know how to, and you are fonder of James's fingernail than you are of me. What's a fellow to do when his wife doesn't love him but drink and play cards? Will you tell me that?"[8] A somewhat agreeable statement, but it is still depressing to see such bland wants for happiness and excuses for vices as being Harry’s supernova. Of Jean-Benoit, however, Dona is often embarrassed by how well he can know her by his mere seeking. She seeks Jean-Benoit to be her lover. Beyond the moral qualms one should have, the solution Dona pursues is not entirely sound. First of all, Dona is still a woman. She can go on adventures and help the pirates certainly, but when it comes to playing the part of the man, she barely passes. The men of the merchant vessel believe her boyhood on the absurdity in which it is presented to them, and though they do not know it they manage to see right through her disguise and tell something is wrong. She does not manage to fish and she is not able to manage the sails and the like with the rest of the crew; she only manages to keep the ship steady at the wheel, the most detached aspect of sailing. She clings hard to this, but it is not enough – and it is not as though she should be incapable of this on account of being a woman anyway. It would not really be difficult for a woman in real life to do any of these things, given the same circumstances that the men were given, but Dona's failure in these respects is representative of the failures of the solution to her problems, shows that her solution might give her something but is severely lacking. Secondly, this is not a permanent solution. Even Jean-Benoit admits that his piracy cannot go on forever: "I could sail away now in La Mouette and come back to you in twenty years' time, and what should I find but a placid, comfortable woman in place of my cabin-boy, with her dreams long forgotten, and I myself a weather-beaten mariner, stiff in the joints, with bearded face, and my taste for piracy gone with the spent years."[9] Chasing your dreams is fun in the moment, but it isn't really living. The thing they fight against is reality itself, something that cannot be overcome by man, let alone a woman in the seventeenth century. This desire to be free is not just about what the world is, but it is what the world is plus who they are. Life is not going to give them anything. Even Jean-Benoit's dreams are, at that moment, being thrwarted; even his own solution, much more stalwart than Dona's, is now crumbling and fading away. One does not ever find their dreams blossoming in life, not their first dreams, but will see the life the Lord has given them and sow upon the ground the Lord has led them to cultivate, which of course is the ground we all find ourselves on now, and this is what Jean-Benoit accepts: he is outdoing life in some little sense now, but nature will win eventually, and he will mature into a man of his time, much as Dona will mature into a woman of her time.

The book does not ends with Dona and Jean-Benoit sailing away together. The Frenchman himself, when he has the countrymen his prisoner, admits to Dona, "I realized that our make-believe was over, and the creek was our sanctuary no more. From this time forward the La Mouette must sail other waters, and find different hiding-places. And although she will be free, and the men on board her free, her master will remain captive."[10] After the pirate's escape from imprisonment, the two talk together for the final time, and Dona cannot help but agree in her own circumstances:

"'But it was different, what I did that night. At first I was afraid, and then I was angry, and when I was angry I took the shield down from the wall, and afterwards – he died.'
"'What made you angry?' he asked.
"She thought a moment, trying to remember, and then, 'It was James, [her son]' she said, 'it was James who woke and cried'. . .
'Ah,' he said, 'so it was James who woke and cried, and you and I, Dona, we meet at the Loe pool instead of Coverack, and your answer is the same as mine.'"[11]

Dona sees and accepts the reality of her situation, and accepts it on its face. Regardless of what was gained and what was lost, what she has and what she does not have, her life has played out a certain way and that is all. Nonetheless, our mistress has no regret for the excursion she had but rather more informed about life than she was before: she has now a more nuanced and expansive knowledge of her role in life, and she is aware of the cruel reality of her sitaution and has accepted her helplessness in it. She does not resign herself but decides to move forward knowing these things. The novel ends with her deciding to stay in her old life, but it details none of what that actually looks like.

Practical alternative solutions in Dona's time are limited; she is a woman in seventeenth century England. It is easy for a woman today to live alone and do what she wants to, even with children, but Dona does not have the luxuries of modern medicine or supermarkets or machinery of any sort or transportation besides wooden ships and carriages, so on and so forth. At this time in history, the family structure exists primarily to allow people to be able to care for themselves and not be overwhelmed by the amount of labor that goes into keeping one's self alive. What one should really do in response to this kind of thing and to our world at large, of course, is look upon that which is whole, that which does function within itself perfectly, that which has no derangement of function, in a word, the Lord. One cannot expect this action to relieve one of the feeling of being misaligned in their roles and world; in fact, turning one's life to Christ will make one realize the true depths of the world's depravity, for if it is so distressing to realize one's place as a woman is to be oppressed, how much more distress is to be added upon seeing mankind's abuse of the creature and the world, the cold back we give to each other in our darkest moments, the way we pile dirt and rubbish on top of anything beautiful the moment we find it out, how we care none for the longevity and well being of the world we were created to take care of, most of all the abject obstinate nature of it all, our powerless to affect anything once we set upon it with our own being, knowing that we have instead willingly participated in all of this and made it our life's end to propagate it. If what one seeks is to feel better about their place in the world, they will only find themselves running away from the Christ in terror. The Lord is foremost a Lord of truth, and this is ultimately what pulls everyone, anyone to Him. Consider the fourth chapter of James. Its main theme is submission to Christ in all circumstances. It opens by admonishing those who seek for things outside themselves through violent means, for, as Eugene Peterson phrases it, "You wouldn't think of just asking God for it, would you? And why not? Because you know you'd be asking for what you have no right to."[12] More directly, beyond all things, that life of a man is something Dona has no right to. The Lord is not coming into your life to drag you out of some hole or something. He is the Triune God, Creator of all things known and unknown, et cetera, and in His abounding love and great mercy He has made Himself known to us so that we may be able to turn our backs on the sinful nature inside of us something we are incapable of without the indwelling of the Holy Spirit. We cannot boast that we shall do this thing or that thing, have no claim to power over our lives as believers nor any truth in the ultimate permanence of things as they are. We submit all things to the Lord and acknowledge His power over all things. If we have come unto the Lord and do not like where we are, that is shameful, and we do not have any right to rebel against the will of the Lord. He has made Himself present in us for comfort, and all of our woes and complaints we have we may take to Him. What Dona should or should not do is important, in fact defining of the Christian life, but it is secondary, for Christ did not call us primarily to do things but primarily to have faith in Him, and without this beginning all else will fail abjectly. We must remember to consult the Lord in all we do and hold fast to Him regardless of our circumstances, knowing He is sovereign over His Creation and is leading us where He would have us be. In all things we must be humble and submit unto Christ's will, and Christ, as we turn towards Him, will draw near to us. If we do not humble ourselves to the uttermost, we shall never be exalted; all things must start here, on our face before the Lord.
