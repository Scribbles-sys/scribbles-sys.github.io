Biography & Volatileness

Return to catalog

Christ is our firm foundation. [Ecclesiastes 3, 5; Isaiah 28; Matthew 7 (Luke 6:17-49); 1 Corinthians 3] Without him we are crazed projectiles careening through life on a path of death and destruction; only the coolness of Christ will quiet us. By the end of the Great War, the world was unconcerned with any kind of cooling. So are the characters of Biography. The play is so named because the point of interest is a biography the main character, Marion, is writing. This act concerns a lot of people – the hateful fiery Richard Kurt, the timid and uncertain Leander Nolan, the powerful and charming Orrin Kinnicott, and Marion herself – along with some others. These people are, in a word, unhinged. Without the foundation of Christ, they crumble. Every way apart from Christ is indeed apart from Christ, and this life has its own consequences here despite any material gain, but it is also true that some lives apart from Christ are better than other lives apart from Christ; Biography shows all of this through it's portrayal of the coming new age.

The most ridiculous of people is Leander Nolan, an old friend of Marion's and an aspiring politician. At his introduction, we are introduced to Nolan's back-and-forth nature as he flashes between entranced with his childhood friend and furious with her for her present. Their initial conversation starts out in sweet and playful tone:

"NOLAN. I came here, Marion, in a perfectly sincere mood, to say something to you, something that's been on my mind ever since we parted, but if you're going to be flippant I suppose there's no use my saying anything – I might as well go, in fact. [But he makes no attempt to do so.]"[1]

But turns rather quickly, "[irritated beyond control]. I wish you wouldn't call me Bunny. . . ."[2] The matter Nolan wants to discuss is Marion's promiscuous lifestyle – that is, she hooks up with Hollywood men a lot. Nolan is running for Senate and is to be married in a month and is fearful of the image he might have in both areas should it be known he was once good friends with the sexual loose cannon that Marion is. This may seem odd to the modern reader, us living well after the pleasure-sexual philosophies of the Europeans, whose academic appeal was only just forming at the time period of this play, the 1930s, and living in a world callous to political primaries – for the latter, consider the recent Maine primary, where Graham Platner won 72% of the Democrat Party vote.[3] This is after greatly publicized controversies for his Schutzstaffel tattoo and him admitting explicitly to having sexual relations through texts with six women other than his wife.[4] It may seem odd to care about something stupid like a childhood friend having a lot of sex with famous people, but Nolan's worry is valid in his time. Of course, Marion goes on with her biography, and Nolan is quite upset: "Opportunity! Cheap exhibitionism! [Indicating magazine on piano] I won't be drawn into it. I can tell you that! [He is in a towering rage.];" again, though, just one moment later: "[scared now that he sees the extent to which he has hurt her]. Marion! . . ."[5] Nolan has no morals he sticks to, neither to Marion, as is obvious, but nor to the Kinnicotts – his yearning for Marion is so strong that he gives up both his oncoming marriage with Slate and forgoes his Senate campaign altogether. Marion rejects him, of course, as she has done away with the writing, feeling it isn't worth dealing with all of these men over – that, plus she is having harder feelings for someone else. The end of the play still has Nolan say "Marion, I know I seem stupid and ridiculous to you – just a Babbit – clumsy – but I love you Marion. I always have – never anyone else" in the one moment, honest and vulnerable in earnest it seems, and fifteen lines later be as "You – you. . . [He feels like striking her, controls himself with difficulty.] Anybody else but him. . . !"[6] His instability seems to have only grown worse, in spite of everything else – or perhaps because he is forcing himself to be more honest about the person he really is, unwilling to compromise on his flaws. The other person Marion intends to be in love with is Richard Kurt, of course.

Richard Kurt's opposition to Nolan is only on basis of external factors, with perhaps some mild competition at play. They obviously enjoy playing their game, but it is difficult to tell to whether their want for vacant nonsense or their genuine stupidity contribute more to their conflict; the pair are, in reality, quite similar. Kurt takes quite an anti-establishment point of view from the very beginning – this is why he views Marion highly, whose lifestyle would have been very "anti-establishment" so to speak – but other than this, Kurt shares much with Nolan, especially his love for Marion and his inability to remain in one moral or mental place. Most emblematic of this is his confession. Marion, as a stranger, initially is only a means to Kurt's end, a way for him to score a hit, both in his career and against the society he so hates. Marion has a strange way about her, though – she was able to come close enough to Mussolini to do a portrait of him, even – and Kurt falls for her just as Nolan did.

"KURT [hard and ironic]. It's trivial really. People exaggerate the importance of human life. One has to die. [Turns to her] The point is to have fun while you're alive, isn't it? Well, you've managed, I congratulate you!
MARION [her heart full]. Dickie darling – why are you so bitter against me? Why against me. . . ?
KURT. Do you want to know that, too? Well, it's because. . . [His voice rises. She suddenly doesn't want him to speak.]
MARION. Hush, dearest – hush – don't say anymore – I understand – not any more. . .
[His defenses vanish suddenly. He sinks to his knees beside her, his arms around her.]
KURT. Marion, angel!"[7]

Kurt is the one who convinces Marion to write the autobiography. He wants to show her lifestyle to the world – not because he believes in it necessarily, but because he believes in the destruction of the current system, which Marion's lifestyle does seek to do. His hatred is blind and unfeeling, so he takes advantage of Marion's circumstance for his own gain. As was just demonstrated, blind and unfeeling people are very easy to order around, too; Marion has him fall for her effortlessly, and she benevolently feels pity for him as her plaything.

Orrin Kinnicott makes a late appearance, but he too changes quite quickly, in spite of his assertion that "I haven't changed my ideas in twenty-five years."[8] He arrives at the beginning of Act 3, intending to help Nolan squash the publication of Marion's biography for fear of public image. He comes in to Marion's life showing the full force of his political might, threatening both Marion and Kurt with great harm to their lives politically. Then he and Marion are allowed some alone time. Kinnicott's disarming is quick, of course: "[definitely aware now of MARION as a personal possibility] I can see this, Marion – if you'd met me – instead of Leander – when you were a young girl – you'd have been a different woman"[9] He is summarily sent away, the powerful man serving only to showcase Marion's own great power. He comes in to affect something that only Marion can affect, and she affects it simply and quietly.

That power is elusive in Marion Froude, even perhaps to herself. She is the hero of this play; the spineless men she has to interact with are entirely at her mercy; she is not bound to "the system" by neither Nolan's submission to it nor Kurt's hatred to it. She describers herself simply and adequately in a conversation with Kurt:

". . .And yet you flirt and curry favor and bandy with them. And you're amused – always amused!
MARION. Yes. I am amused,"[10]

Marion lives her life as she likes it, chasing after her fancies. Regrettably, this is not different from the other characters, despite her well-exercised superiority to them. Consider Marion's assessment of Kinnicott's sweethearted moment: "He thinks tomorrow night he might cajole me – through the exercise of his great personal charm – into giving up my plan to publish."[11] Of course, that event stands as Kinnicott's submission into a lack of foundation, but it is difficult to tell with what sincerity Marion says things like this, or if she's correct regardless; on the one hand, Marion is, moreso than most of the rest of the cast, honest and stalwart in morals; on the other hand, she has knowingly and deliberately charmed Kinnicott with her honest and stalwart demeanor. Marion is also much a victim of the people in her life at this point in time, recall. The failure of everyone else to use her quite like they wanted to, to subject them to their own mercilessly volatile natures, does not negate their actions. Either way, she cancels that dinner with Orrin a few minutes later, using Nolan as a proxy to do so. Then there is the almost heartbreaking final lines she utters: "No, Minnie – no one – we travel alone!"[12] So she abandons the entirety of the cast – who have only dragged her down anyway – to return to her flippant lifestyle. She just before this expresses some sorrow at her plight: "Yes, I know. But it's possible one can pay too much for independence. I'm adrift,"[13] but ultimately she indulges in it. Her amusement is far more important than the other things. Everyone's amusement, their temper, their instant framing of their life, is more important to them than any other thing; the conflicts of this play stem entirely in this. Marion's returns to chasing Hollywood men is, almost comedically, the only sign of consistency in the play. It is the only instance of someone sticking to something. This gives Marion some advantage, gives her power; in a world of sloth, just barely trying exalts her. Nonetheless, her superiority remains destruction, primarily for herself but also for others. She is merely the most important playing piece in the world's explosive games. It is a welcome, sought after destruction for everyone; this is worse than if it had come blind in the night. How much worse the land becomes if the tyrants and the rebels pilfer it side by side with glee.

It must also be said that, in the grand scheme of things, no one changes at all. They are satellites of the great Lord; though they change their place in orbit around Him, never do they draw closer, and drawing closer is the only movement that really matters, the only direction worth traversing. Of course, no satellite on its own would ever choose to break the orbit of the one it encircles in order to be closer; only the force of the central object acting on those in orbit can bring them closer, and it is only in abandoning the loop that you know and descending directly into the furnace that one comes closer to Christ. Not all suffering is Christ, of course – these New York figures suffer quite a lot – but one should expect much greater suffering when they serve a greater God, a God who seeks to fulfill your humanity and not cast it to vanities. Those who seek only to be free of the star, to unburden themselves of the demands of Christ and seek the life of a freed man, will leave the orbit of the star and discover the eternal darkness. No one is innocent in this regard. We are all volatile and drifting, with no guiding light. Only Christ can establish the orbits that define us and make us truly, fully human eradicating the shallow depth that comes with a flippant regard for love and life and the idiocy that leaves one unable to deal with life ahead of the second in which it is lived. May Christ have mercy on our souls.
