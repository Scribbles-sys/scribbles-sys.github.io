Dinner at Antoine's & Instability

Return to catalog

Dinner at Antoine's has a lot going on but is a whodunit at its core. A wife, Odile, ill with Parkinson's disease, dies of a gunshot wound, and near everyone's a suspect. The wife herself is among them; she had ample motive to kill herself. Her mother, Amelie, is mentally unwell and felt stuck taking care of her; Odile's husband, Leonce, is lecherous and was having an affair with the wife's sister, Caresse, the latter who was quite jealous of Odile; Sabin is a gruntled ex-lover who is the one who gave Odile the gun used to kill her. There is Amalie's suitor, Orson, who was willing to stage a coup in Latin America to help his company survive, so how much more kill an ill daughter so he can marry Amelie. Then there's Odile's physician, Perrault, who always cared for her and gave her his best to help her overcome her illness. Naturally, it turns out Perrault was the one who killed Odile. Perrault felt he was responsible for Odile attempting suicide, and so he killed her before she could kill herself to spare her the mortal sin. In a confession letter, Perrault writes, "I began to think clearly again, to understand that I was responsible for the mortal sin which Odile was about to commit. And I knew that whatever else might happen, I must take that sin from her and upon myself."[1] Turning from Christ will always lead to such, regardless of how put together one's life can be aside from the Lord. Christ is the cornerstone of all life direction. The Lord is the source of all ethics and mores. The Holy Spirit is the genesis of salvation. To try to progress without Christ, to construct morality without the Lord, to save oneself aside from the Holy Spirit; all of these things blaspheme the Triune God, regardless of how much they appear to resemble biblical living. It will lead one to kill someone else so they might make it into Heaven. Turning from Christ makes the world unstable, not only in the minds of the people but in the homes and streets of the world.

The Lalande's are the epitome of instability. As noted, they are a very ecstatic family. Odile, as unfortunate of a victim as she is, is among like company. Recall that Odile had, in fact, intended to commit suicide. A treatment for Odile's condition is found just six months after her death, through Benadryl. This is, of course, a great irony to Perrault, who said, as Amalie relates to Orson, "Vance came out and told Odile, in my presence, that her case was hopeless. . . That she would become increasingly helpless, and there was nothing anyone could do about it, because that type of paralysis or palsy was one of the afflictions for which no one could find a cure."[2] This is also a great irony to Odile, who feared not being able to live her whole life in her state when relief would have arrived in a year or so, most likely. Consider what Philip Yancey writes of Brian Sternberg: "Yet, even that position [that the Lord will heal Brian's paralysis in this lifetime], which seems more and more insupportable to Brian's friends, is a definite turning to God. Brian has learned to trust and believe in a loving, worthy God despite torment which few will ever experience."[3] Even if Odile was never granted relief, Christ was there to comfort her and in fact was crucified, dead, buried, and rose from the dead specifically so He could comfort Odile and everyone in their afflictions caused by the Fall; even though this is true, waiting on a relief which one knows to be in Christ's nature to provide is not to turn against Christ but to draw nearer to Him, and Odile would have been rewarded. Odile had express path and opportunity to turn to Christ and denied it. She preferred the brutal cyclone of worldly misery as opposed to the stability Christ. Christ would have been the completion in Odile's torment, but Odile rejected completeness. The rest of her family did as well as mentioned, as did the monstrous Orson. He is the president of a shipping company and is attempting to take over a competitor company by any means diplomatic or violent. Consider a brief part of his reflection of his rant towards Amelie:

"Most all, he should not have said that people who lived in glass houses must not throw stones, that if he had not told her where he was last Saturday night [the night of the murder,] neither was there anything to prove she had not slipped out of her bedroom and into her daughter's. . .
"No, he should not have said any of these things and, after a lapse of time, he would find a way of admitting this, indirectly, to Amelie."[4]

A reflection that admits fault and finds no problem with having fault. To the end Orson continues in this way, even as he falls for the Francisco daughter for her mutual appreciation of him. His turning from Christ needs no restating.

Instability is not always so outlandish and loud. Compared to everyone else, Ruth Avery, Orson's niece, could perhaps be called moral, but she is certainly fickle, too. It is indicated from early on that her and Russell Aldridge, an archaeologist and proper adventurer, will come together by the end of the novel, not only to the reader but to Ruth herself. Yet, Ruth still considers Sabin as a possible lover once it becomes clear a relationship with Russell will not be easy and smooth. Her heart yearns for Russell, and yet she still entertains others. As the narrator states, "Ruth's intuition told her that an association with Sabin might well be as dangerous as it would be delightful; but she was in no mood to listen to reason; she was ready to welcome anything that assuaged her hurt pride, anything that might soothe her sore heart."[5]Of course, perhaps this is not true infidelity, but the manner of Ruth's heart indicates she is looking for some kind of betrayal on her own to take part in, a kind of revenge. Ruth only considers Russell properly once he responds to her again through a radiogram in Peru and explicitly asks for her hand in marriage, and, given the extenuating circumstances, they marry in Peru, where his work has taken him and where he spends weeks at a time in caves searching for artifacts. It may seem incorrect for Ruth to have married in this way, and an outside look in makes all the matches in this novel seem quite doomed, but they are not doomed on account of shrugging off modern notions of marriage. Ruth's main dilemma was her lack of devotion for that which was set for her to begin with, that which she knew she was willing to sacrifice for. How much do we play the Ruth when it comes to the Lord. On her own, with no external guidance, Ruth does not have the surety she needs to simply do what she knows is the best for everyone.

Some instability does not seem to show itself at all. Perrault is of this kind. He is not merely visibly secure but has a healthy relationship with his community. Consider his speech towards Caresse when she falls ill on visiting Odile's tomb: "I'm rather tired, as you say. I've felt the need of a rest for quite a while. But as long as Odile depended on me. . . . My other patients don't matter so much. Except you."[6] This nice way of talking even after he murdered Odile. It's not any mystery how such a man could commit such an act. He forsakes it all because he attempts to build rapport without that which all security in life depends on, the Messiah. Everyone else views themselves as Perrault views himself: both believing themselves to be fully complete as isolated individuals and being able to openly admit their flaws and their vices without seeing them as a contradiction towards their perfect stance. Perrault is just the one who comes closest to visualizing this opinion of himself to everyone else; Perrault is not different from the rest of the cast, not any worse nor any better. Russelll is also of this kind. He seems to be pretty well off; he is academic but the scholar who thrives in the field; he is well mannered but not snobbish. In this manner he appears to embody completeness as human beings seek it. When Ruth's stepfather, Richard, comes to talk with Orson about his Latin Amrican escapades, Ruth asks the former his advice on Russell, and Richard says 'I don't know a single thing to Aldridge's discredit. He’s about as fine as they come. . .he took it for granted that you'd understand. That shows how he felt about you. If he hadn't admired you tremendously, if he hadn't loved you greatly, he wouldn't have been so sure he could count on you."[7] He is nice and caring and considerate and faithful to Ruth. And then he leaves her for his job. He leaves her because he, too, suffers from the debilitation that Ruth has. Not to suggest that Russell should not have gone to Peru, but rather open and honest communication from him would have made things better for both, and as Richard mentions, Russell deliberately puts that idea down. It is graceful the two could come together as they do at the end of the novel.

Not all of New Orleans is doomed; some do in fact have stability: the Racina's. We mainly see Joe and Judith Racina through Caresse's eyes, as they help her land a better job for a conservative fashion store in New York et cetera. Their care for her is greater than even the other character's epiphany's bring them to, but most fascinating and most telling is Joe and Judith's home life. The presence of Christ in their life is far too thin, but even this proves to be a light to all of those around them. The narrator notes,

"Her [Judith's] perfect contentment, her complete sense of well-being and fulfillment were manifest in every word she spoke. Caresse swallowed hard, trying not to let jealousy creep into her own renewed feeling of hopefulness and courage. But it seemed to her that Judith had everything – at least everything that mattered: a husband whom she loved and who loved her; two fine children and the prospect of another; a home that was not merely pleasant but happy; adequate if not ample means for every material requisite."[8]

Caresse still focuses on a hedonistic view of life but cannot help but notice that the two are secure and set apart. We can see a number of Bibles the Racinas own, and we see the Racina's new baby "duly entered in the old family Bibles as Caresse Lalande Racina" and Joe "for some moments turning these pages of records and studying them with interest. . ."[9] Despite Joe's rejection, Christ is with him and looking upon him and Joe appears to be responding. Christ is with their family too, of course. Consider too the left-out Lalande herself. Caresse starts off the novel in an awful way, being playful with her sister's husband and full of jealousy of Odile having Leonce. However, Caresse grows tired of Leonce's games and feels sick for even being with him, and focuses less on others and more on herself, which leads her to want to take a job at a conservative fashion store in New York. In spite of her being a suspect on Saturday, by the next Saturday she is thankfully cleared to pursue her own life and not attach herself meaninglessly to her family; as horrifying as the prospect of ditching one's kin is, how much more horrifying it must be to stay enslaved to a heathen family, and how wrong it would be to choose them over Christ, even if Caresse still caters to her underlying current of godless isolation by the end of the novel. Caresse shows that the way that most of the rest of the characters are is not necessarily just the way they are; they are all capable of change should they ever truly value righteousness and purity.

We have a look at everyone's lives after Perrault's suicide, even the detective's life. Amelie finds a new suitor, Russelll and Ruth live a strained and separated but happy marriage, Clarinda and Orson marry well enough, Leonce continues to be a swinger and continues to detest his wife in his mind, the Racinas stay the same, Caresse makes a good life in New York, Sabin flies radar planes for Orson's company. Some people do end up in better spots than they were before, and some are merely doing the same things they were before. Most still give the sense of being lost. This is because their lives are like kaleidoscopes: abstract, artificial, meaningless except for the rare bouts of empty hedonism. This is the cause of their issues. Many may say to seek for things reliant upon themselves and to seek a permanent substance is the root of all sin, but this is not true. The Lord is the source of change and progress, and life rejecting Him necessarily is stagnant; do not believe that such change is the source of instability. [Numbers 23:13-26; Isaiah 43; James 1:1-12; Jude] The problem with the characters of Dinner at Antoine's isn't that they move about at all or too much, but that they are all satellites ejected from their orbits and drift without meaning in empty space. It is the guiding force of a sun that allows the motion of our planets to be meaningful; similarly, letting go of everything and seeking true spiritual entropy will not solve the issue of stagnancy but will serve as its completion; it is the form for those movements we have been designed for which is what we need, and Christ is the only acceptable guide.
