To The Sea & Godhood

Return to catalog

"I just want to be human." Hikari says this often throughout To the Sea. She is a member of the Triton tribe, protectors of the sea who have historically fought against humans' destruction of the oceans. Luca, the dolphin who summons Hikari and informs her of her Triton status, gives her an unbearable responsibility, godhood of the sea; she just wants to live a normal life, with a mother and a father and wind ensemble and homework. Hikari does land her wish, only as a final ultimatum to her horrible odyssey. We live a life much like that: you and I are not human. We are subhuman. Life requires more than subhumanity of us, demands us to restore it even though nothing would allow us to. There has only ever been one Man, the Son of Man, Jesus Christ. [Mark 11: 32-42; Colossians 2; 1 John 1] Only He was fully man; we are missing many parts. Everyone recognizes this; this is the basis of all heretic ideologies; the ancient Gnostics and the transhumanist philosophers of today share this same ultimate goal, to become human, but both fall short because they assume such an uplift could ever exist outside of the Holy Spirit, that something other than Christ could have the power to uplift us from our subhuman existence. Often they even lose sight of their humanity to begin with and seek godhood. Such is the fate of Hikari and Heptapoda in To the Sea, and naturally this does not go well for them. Humanity's attempt to uplift itself is quite obviously a worthless endeavor, for only Christ can execute this good plan.

The vague antagonist of this story, the "leader" of the Poseidon tribe, the humans, is Heptapoda. She is no president nor minister; she is just a girl. She is a genius, though. She also believed she was a Triton. Luca came for her before seeking out Hikari. Heptapoda is the daughter of a prostitute; sex work is her family profession, in fact, and the girls are required to start young. Heptapoda finds an orichalcum blade, which Luca asks for as proof of her Tritonhood. In our world, orichalcum is an ancient brass alloy, one that many associated with Atlantis. In To the Sea, orichalcum is the property of the Triton tribe, a substance with godlike powers in the hands of the knowledgeable. Only the Tritons can use this substance, so having it in one’s possession is proof of being a Triton. Heptapoda uses the blade to free herself from a cllient her parents put on her. Once she is free, though, Heptapoda finds she is not a Triton after all, and her magical, all-powerful blade is merely a knife. She is freed of her servitude by her own means – of framing her parents as having robbed and killed that man, and using an American initiative for geniuses to escape her country – but it isn't important to her. "But I no longer cared about any of that. I was no longer able to go out to sea."[1] Her situation had not truly been satisfied. Thus starts Heptapoda's obsession. She is eventually led to lead a secret project for the United States, one that is similarly obsessed with orichalcum – to the point of having satellites dedicated to tracing the substance. The United States wants to use the power of orichalcum to hopefully revert the melting of the Northern ice caps, but Heptapoda secretly just wants the power of the blade. Nothing will stop her from this endeavor. See this play out as she kills her own caretaker on the project: 'You. . . let the journalist that I told you to get rid of live, didn't you? I had to take care of the journalist on my own. It seems you are too sentimental and curious for your own good. You aren't someone who can make it as a professional in this world. And that has a possibility of becoming a thread that could lead to danger for us in the future."[2] Thoughts of escape, of fairness, of progress, of improvement, of repair, of any such thing evaporate from Heptapoda's mind. She is only concerned with the power of godhood that real Tritons have, Tritons like Hikari. Hikari eventually hands over the orichalcum; once Heptapoda obtains the blade, she turns her back on the United States and immediately enacts her own plan: to freeze not the Arctic Ocean, but the entire Global North. This is laid out in her conversation with a lead on the Arctic project:

"The principal advanced nations in the middle and high latitude areas will suffer the most serious damage, but many of the developing nations in the lower latitudes will be left."
"Ridiculous! The leading nations aren't the ones that are supposed to be suffering the most! Through the wars, famines, and disasters that have occurred, it was the most advanced nations that kept losses minimal through their wealth. . . "
"If the most advanced nations still try to continue being the leaders of the Earth, I shall just have to rectify that situation."
"You think you're a god now?"
"A god. . .? Hmm, yes, I might be something close to that."[3]

Heptapoda has made herself arbiter of the world, has used her human genius to save it, and is unable to affect anything. Her actions are meaningless. They are meaningless for Hikari.

Our hero, the protagonist, is Hikari, the Triton. Hikari is quite similar to Heptapoda. They're both young girls from Southeast Asia, though Heptapoda is a bit younger. Hikari starts as a member of the Tritons, and she is called to take a journey across the ocean, throughout the world, in order to save the oceans from the destructive nature of humans. Hikari is absolutely certain, though, that she is a human. She is certain, too, at first, that humans are not as cruel as Luca makes them out to be. Hikari uses the orichalcum blade early on; though she is not in any immediate danger, she nonetheless stabs her mother and runs off to join Luca. She later uses the blade to extort services out of a doctor – Luca has become injured, and Hikari has no one else to turn to. "It was unexpected, but the doctor asked me where the patient was and began to quickly prepare for an examination. That's right. . . there are many good humans in this world, too. Of course, there are"[4] The doctor haphazardly looks at the wound and does nothing to it, and then: "How about I take the knife in exchange. . . and now. . . the knife's not enough. You'll have to make some money with your body for a few months, and then we’ll call it even."[5] It is now that Hikari conjures the true power of orichalcum, manifesting it away from the man and back to her, scaring the doctor off in the process. Why so much less spectacle than with Heptapoda's godhood? It is even worse when the supposedly dead journalist interfering with Heptapoda survives and rejoins the scene. He finds Hikari helpless and helps her restart her journey. He is set on finding the truth about the project and what the Americans want – that is, until he realizes that Hikari is a helpless little girl that he can do whatever he wants with, and that he can film them having sex and sell the videos to make a living. This goes on for some time, until this is what completely envelops the journalist. It is only then that Hikari breaks out. This time, she uses great force, blowing up buildings, leaping and slicing through a chinook, et cetera. Hikari could have used the power of orichalcum – even greater power than this – to stop her being used right from the start. This would have mattered greatly to herself, of course, but the godhood she has been pampered in to is not considered with such small things – that is, no one tries to break the bonds of their human nature in order to watch fireflies gather more closely or to be able to pet dogs better. This requires full humanity, not the godhood constructed here, the godhood that robbed Hikari of all agency and direction. The journey she has been put upon is directionless and without a goal. It is nihil. She has become nihil. Eventually, Hikari surrenders. When the Americans finally capture Hikari after her surrender, and place her with her mother who has also been interrogated, they seek Hikari's help, her knowledge, to both further the original Arctic project and to stop Heptapoda. Hikari has nothing to offer. One man important comes in and furiously picks Hikari up and shakes her around, screaming "WE KNOW YOU TWO ARE UP TO SOMETHING! DON'T YOU KNOW HOW MANY PEOPLE DIED BECAUSE OF YOU!? TELL ME EVERYTHING YOU KNOW!!"[6] Hikari simply cries, balks, runs away in fear. She doesn't know anything. Being a Triton wasn't a conscious decision by her, not something she wants, and only twice has she ever directly called upon the power of orichalcum, both times to save her from sexual abuse, nothing to do with her supposed mission of saving the oceans. Hikari is helpless. The man leaves. This is right after Hikari gives the blade to Heptapoda. Heptapoda, in her mania, also cannot understand Hikari's unwillingness to act, cannot fathom Hikari’s supposed powerlessness as a Triton:

"But if you want this blade, Heptapoda, then I’ll give it to you."
"No, that's not. . . we want you to fight with us to save this world, Hikari-san."
"Here. The orichalcum blade belongs to you now. . .
"I'm going back to just being a regular human Umino Hikari."[7]

Hikari leaves too, once Heptapoda has gone on her spree. Heptapoda confronts Hikari, unable to determine what it is she wants, even though she has spelled it out so clearly to everyone she meets for the length of this work:

"Even if you're just a regular human, I'll [Heptapoda'll] protect you. Let's live here together."
"We're going home."
"Huh?"
"We're going back to our home."[8]

It is no wonder as to why Hikari forgoes the blade. She seeks her humanity. Hikari maintains her humanity in the face of Luca's revalation that the Tritons fight against the humans and therefore must not be human and Heptapoda's confusion over Hikari's powerlessness. She maintains her humanity even as she herself enacts violence in order to further her own construction of godhood. The advantages of godhood are null; there are no superhumans, no strength to overcome life's challenges, no knowledge to help solve one's problems, there is no advantage that godhood offers you. It can help you escape a situation, perhaps, but it would not have saved you from its completion nor its existence, much less from the world and its circumstances which created that situation. Nonetheless, Hikari holds out on her power to fulfill her wish right unto the end. It is not long after Heptapoda's offer before Hikari succumbs to the freezing weather and to exhaustion. Hikari does not lose all of her power, though. She is granted one last thing as she falls onto the ice – the thing she wanted all along, to be a normal girl. She wakes up in class, and some of her other classmates take her to go visit the dolphin, just as her story starts out. So then, was all that happened to Hikari a mere dream, nothing at all? Of course not. Hikari immediately runs to Luca:

"Luca. . . I wanted to see you again so much."
"It's good that you were able to make it back to Japan."
"Yeah. . . but what's going on here?"
"This is what you wished for. It happened because you’re a part of the Triton tribe. . ."
What will you do? Will you continue to wield the blade?"[9]

In her dreams, Hikari recalls pushing beyond everything nonsensically, recalls her transient world, recalls holding on to her journey tightly, but wakes up holding what she had wanted all along, her plush dolphin Finn, in her bed with her computer.

Peter Neill defines provolution as follows:

"It's surely time . . . to imagine a future that incorporates the latest, most volatile circumstances into a design of reality-based actions that will shape forward intent rather than react to the future. It is time to change the way we see the world. It is not time for revolution, an impossible circular return to the past, nor for an evolution, which is just too slow, but it is time for provolution, a collective force forward and through to a new set of values, structures, behaviors, and actions that will correct the negative valence to positive energy and the future."[10]

Consider the revolution Luca so desperately clings to. Luca wants to overthrow human reign, to set things right themselves, to use the power of the orichalcum to restore the oceans, to bring balance to the world, to restore natural order, to take a fallen world and stand it upright again. It is obvious even to Hikari the inanity of this way of thinking. A revolution must necessarily draw upon the world it seeks to overthrow. Even if it could unencumber itself of the flaws within that, the pool of resources it has to draw from is shallow, insufficient. The world cannot be revolutionized into an Utopia. Consider the evolution Heptapoda tries to enact. She uses her own intelligence and foresight to enact justice and restore balance. She gives an upper hand to the struggling and calls forth a new era, not one of overturning but of progress beyond. It is random and directionless, it is cruel and maniacal, it is hollow and vapid, and it is painfully slow. It would take centuries to even see what the result of all of this would be, and perhaps the result is to just be death by ocean overflow anyway. The world cannot be cultivated into a higher order. Consider the humanity Hikari yearns for. She's not capable of changing the world. She cannot withstand the absurdity of natural metamorphosis. She is only meant for practicing her recorder, for letting her father take the brunt of her school work. She craves the fullness of human experience, one that no Odyssian trek could reproduce, one that no earthly crucible could form. It is something that is absent from her life. The way the world works, the orientation of its mind, the fidelity of its structure, all point to brokenness that cannot be overcame. It needs something to repair, reassemble, make whole again, complete it. It needs Christ. Christ never called for revolution – the Jews crucified Him majorly on this fact. The Lord never called for evolution – humans cannot shepherd creation into eternity themselves and are not commanded to. Only the hand of the Holy Spirit laid upon the surface of the waters can make them pure again. We cannot climb to Heaven, but Christ is just and fair and merciful, even when it seems otherwise, to lift us out of the brokenness of the world we inhabit. Christ will not do away with this world but will restore it, make it new again. Those who repent unto Him, the One all have wronged, will be restored with it.
