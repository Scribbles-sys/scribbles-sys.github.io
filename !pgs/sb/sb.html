Seaside Beta & Yearning

Return to catalog

Everyone feels that they are not complete within themselves. Many people turn to consumption to fill this whole, devising schemes to provide themselves with endless entertainment and grow their stockpiles of product; some people fill the hole with religion, seeking prayers, idols to fixate on, opinions to regurgitate; some people seek after love, a spouse or many lovers, children to love or a partner to pamper; there are many things one could seek to feel more fulfilled. One could say, "Shouldn't one seek God?" There are two issues with this. First and foremost, one does not seek God, rather one is called. The Holy Spirit is not some obscure text to be rediscovered but a living and active Lord who dwells within the heirs of Heaven. The process of salvation and living life to draw near to Christ is a separate phenomenon to "feeling fulfilled." Consider Nagi Kurokawa, who staunchly believes in a God who is willing to intervene on her behalf and that cares about her fulfillment. She says to Professor Shiraishi, "This world revolves around the story that God created. If we can remember what happens next, then we will know how to return to the other world. As long as we keep praying, God will answer one day. I know he will, because he is a kind god…"[1] Shortly after this, Nagi does receive some divine intervention. That intervention does not tell her what happens next, nor does it send her to the "other world," the real world, not in the way Nagi or anyone else wants it to. Until the end of the work, and after the end of the work too, perhaps, Nagi does not go through life with a sense of fulfillment but with an increased sense of longing. This is the second issue with "seeking God:" all who do ultimately seek Him will not be more satisfied with life but far less satisfied with it. It is only in this that one can live a true life. This seeking is representative of the life of all believers – we are created to be near, but we make it there, after all, by drawing near. This life is not about being near but drawing near, not about being fulfilled but seeking ultimate fulfillment. This is the world of Seaside Beta. The work is a complicated work, with metafictional elements, but ultimately it is about characters in a comic book being endowed with something like full humanity by separate reader characters. This is not something surprising to the characters the comic focuses on; in fact, this is the very thing those characters so desperately want.

Seaside Beta is obsessed with color. In their manga world, there is no color, but three people – Ikebi, Nagi, and Shiraishi – can see brief flashes of primary colors anyway. This ignites a fire in all three of them, and they devote their lives to understanding their comic world better so they can understand this color. What does this color mean, beyond the literal implication of the eye diffusing light reflections? Why the obsession with it, why does color matter enough to warrant that dedication? Knowing all of this, can we conclude that the world of the comic needs this color, or that the characters of the world need it and should chase after it? First, color is "realness," so to speak. Ikebi confesses to Shiraishi, "I'm happy here. I have lots of friends, and I can go anywhere. To be told that it's fake, I should be sad, but. . . even if it means declaring all of the beautiful things here to be fake, I want to go back to that other world."[2] "Color" also extends beyond mere color because it involves a truly supernatural phenomenon, a visitation from someone who seems to mean a lot to Ikebi. Earlier, from before that quote, Shiraishi gives Ikebi some kind of glass cube that contains a conjoined thought bubble. When Ikebi touches it, it sends him into a vision of a young girl with white hair – not colorless hair, but white hair – a white dress shirt with blue accents, and a square blue wristwatch. Shiraishi explains, "The scenery you just saw. . . was the world you're originally from. The world of color."[3] We can take these statements at face value, as while the characters are not exactly right about the true nature of things, the work only reinforces their views; so "color" in this comic is both a state of transcendence and a point of origin. It is not just a higher state of being one obtains to, but a home one returns to. This answer rhetorically answers the other questions posed. These answers; that home is embodied within one's self, it is a place one always wishes to be; that connection is fundamental to directing our choices; that by these qualities alone do matters obtain importance; these answers of course lead themselves to more questions, but the yearning for Color cannot be adequately described or explained in this fundamental way because it is too fundamental. It is simply an aspect of the human condition. Ikebi does not find color and decide to ponder it; Nagi does not decide randomly one day she needs to wait for a messiah; Shiraishi does not pick his studies of the physics of the comics world at random. Color is a natural aspect of the world; the world is defined by its absence. Color is not just who they are, but who they were created to be. Those people who step in to the story, the girl to Ikebi and Nagi's interventionist, that the characters of Seaside Beta fixate on, are also characters within the comic, but they are not a part of Seaside Beta; rather, they are the readers of the comic. There is a related overbearing story within Seaside Beta of four friends, one who draws manga and three others who participate in reading it to various degrees. There is some difficulty in tracing this metastory together, as five names are listed: Suzumi Shion, Shimuya Akane, Ooba, Ao, and Hibana Aoi. The reason why there being five people is an issue is because this creates two problems: (1) There are only four "real" people within this comic, and (2) the real world comics "Alpha from the Nursery" and "Seaside Beta" do not seem to be related in any way, even though characters who would only know about the former are present in the latter. These issues are resolved if we assume that Ao and Suzumi are the same person. There are four reasons we can safely assume this: (1) Ao and Suzumi are at different ages and different points in life, (2) Ao and Suzumi are both introverted and protective of their comics, though Ao moreso, (3) As mentioned, plot points and motifs between “Alpha and the Nursery” and “Seaside Beta” cannot be adequately explained any other way, and (4) There is no refutation by Ao when Ikebi becomes Ao – which we know because the character mentions their legs not working and living at a hospital, things which are only true for Ao – and is called Suzumi by Nagi. These are ultimately assumptions that can be explained away or have their fundamentals challenged. It is always possible that there are five people, that the fifth person is simply not present in Seaside Beta, and that the comic Alpha from the Nursery is related to Seaside Beta in some other way. This seems less likely, as this would set a more distant and surrealist tone than it seems like ohuton is attempting to create. This is a loose assumption, but for the sake of this essay it is being made. These "real people" characters are in this story because they are, in a way, in the story Seaside Beta as its characters. Nagi is not literally Shumiya, but Nagi is a character inspired by and written for Shumiya. Nagi only matters when read by Shumiya – without that interaction, Nagi still lives and has agency, et cetera, but Nagi's life is incomplete without the former's influence, without the former coming in and giving life to Nagi. This can only be done through the act of reading the story. All of the characters in Seaside Beta yearn for a breath of life that is given to them by these characters reading the stories they are in. Even the author, Suzumi, yearns for this life – he thinks this to himself in one of the metanarratives involving Hibana:

"Freedom existed within manga.
With just your imagination, you can make anything and go anywhere.
You can even create things that don’t exist in this world.
While I may not be able to walk, Alpha can go anywhere I want him to.
And I felt like that was enough for me."[4]

There is a privileged position one has as author, and that is to be the most moved by this yearning for color. Ikebi, too, is not literally Suzumi, but is tied to Hibana, and tied to Suzumi. It is them, and everyone who reads, that inspires Ikebi and gives him life. Ao writes for Hibana, and thus it is her reading that is represented to him, her intervention that gives Ikebi his color, his life. Of course, Hibana and Shumiya, et cetera, all have closer relationships to their audience than a book you read will likely have with you, but this still shows the effects of reading. We don't need the reading of these stories in our lives, but nonetheless we love these stories and want to cherish them; rather, it is the stories who need us. It is the general act of reading, reading beyond just absorbing information in written form, that gives life to characters and to a world. As interpreters of a text we become responsible for that text and for its livelihood. There is nothing within the confines of a book that said book's characters can use to give themselves true life, no matter how desperately they wish to have it. This life can only come through the characters via the supernatural intervention of the reader. The reader of a book must graciously bestow life upon the characters within it; the characters' fulfillment, completion, ascension, et cetera, depend entirely upon the reader's want and ability to do this. Should even a meager beam of our interpretation come upon just one wayward leaf falling off of a tree, the work shall know the grace of being made whole; how much more, then, do we feel whole when we are similarly touched by our infallible Creator. This is not necessarily based on an ability to exegize a test with delicate accuracy, although this is perhaps the most superior form of reading, Take Ooba, who cannot understand Suzumi at all:

"'Ooba, is there a person in your life who you would be happy to be able to see tomorrow?'
'Huh?'
Huh?
This guy! I'm amazed he can ask such a question. Are all aspiring authors like this? I'm embarrassed for him."[5]

There is something to be said of Ooba's presence, in the form of Shiraishi in this comic, at all. He doesn't seem to read much of Suzumi's work nor take much interest in its themes; it is beyond Ooba, not something he as interest in. Yet, he does care about Suzumi, and as such necessarily cares about and entertains Suzumi's works. It is this alone that grants him the ability to see color, grants him a place in Seaside Beta. Ooba did not need skill or understanding as much as he needed connection and caring in order to connect with the work and its author; the latter are, in fact, the main point of reading. It is not any success in studying or research, et cetera, that gives one this obsession with color, rather the obsession with color comes upon one, and some choose to chase it in research, some choose to chase it in arts, and some choose to chase it in many other ways. They are not tied intrinsically to color; the yearning is its own thing.

Seaside Beta, then, is not merely a contained work that involves reading, nor is it about reading Seaside Beta only. The beauty of this work is that is is a representative of broader concepts. It is about reading as a general act. Whenever we read any fiction, we do not read anything fabricated but in fact the real world – the author's thoughts and feelings, the author's values and mores, the author's worldview, point of view, biases, perspectives, people and things the author know. A novel, a poem, a play, a short story, all of them are representative of life, but all of them are dead before the reader comes in contact with them. A work on its own without intervention is dead, a death beyond death. This is a representative of the Christian life and shows why reading is not only beneficial but important. In reading, we all become little christs, imperfect christs. Ikebi keeps chasing after the color he sees through material means, through means within the world, and while he learns meaningful things about his world he is brought farther away from color instead of closer to it; it takes that color personally stepping in and grabbing Ikebi, giving him a supernatural way to color; in the same way, we can discover much about our world and its Creator by our own means, but the world can only push us away from Christ; Christ must step in and personally grab us away. Just as Shumiya has to personally come in and tell Nagi where to go, confirm her thoughts which she cannot see by her own vision, so must the Holy Spirit guide us where we need to go, for it is obscured by the world we live in. We come in and give the stories more than they are capable of generating on their own; in the same way, Christ comes into our lives and gives us more than we can have on our own – not more stuff or more emotions or even more humanity necessarily, but more vulnerability, more suffering, more yearning, more introspection. Christ is beyond us, for He is both Author and Reader, both Creator and Interpreter of our own lives, and in a way like Suzumi does, He stepped into His own Work in order to most intimately know it. His supernatural intervention is what makes us closer to being real, and without that stepping in, such reality would be mere rumors to us still. We are mere fiction without Christ, but in drawing nearer to Him we are brought closer to reality.

One can trace a development of thought throughout Christian history, a thought that grows more desperate and agonizing as time progresses. As of time of writing, Christ has been silent for two thousand years. We have been given the gift of Scripture and the indwelling of the Holy Spirit so that we may be shuttled along through this time, gifts we do not deserve. Even our theologies regarding these tend to be attempts to cope with this silence: that Scripture is some kind of transubstantiative apparatus, or that Canon is meaningless and ever-expanding; that the Spirit is somehow physically moving ourselves closer to Christ, or that we have any hope from any source of escaping sin within ourselves in our time on this world. The enduring revelation of Christ's Scripture is not enough for us; if we are honest about what it is and who the Lord is, we would be of strong want to give up. What Christ has given us is not enough; give us more or we walk. This damnation of Christians is an exhibit of our yearning, too. We do not hate our circumstances because we hate Christ but because we yearn for an incorruptible relationship to Him. Christ's silence is painful, perhaps even abhorrent. Many Christians gawk at a Christ who would allow anything "abhorrent," anything not directly synonymous with overwhelming pleasure. We'll say, in our testimonies, that Christ has changed us, has brought dead things of our hearts back to life, has moved mountains, has turned everything around, has opened up the unknowable parts of our souls to us. These are all coping mechanisms. These are all lies. We have not been made together with the Father yet. What we have been given is Color. [Zephaniah 3; Romans 8:18-39; 1 John 1] We have been given yearning for that oneness, have been gifted the ability to seek what is real. We can only seek Christ if we are saved, truly only find Christ if He has found us. All of our work apart from this will be as dust in Christ’s Mercy and be that which secures our damnation in Christ’s Wrath. What we have been given in our salvation is hope, for we know that one day we will be real.
