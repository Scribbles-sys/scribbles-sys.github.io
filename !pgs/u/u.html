Undercurrent & Resolution

Return to catalog

Life is full of loose ends. Loved ones disappear one day and never show up again; feelings evaporate and we can't find where they went to; chronic illness sets in and never dissipates; the rage of a lunatic and their longwithstanding ire towards our homes; et cetera. We have Christ with us in all of this grief. Sometimes the Holy Spirit has mercy upon us and protects us, heals us, directs our loved ones back towards us miraculously, but oftentimes, perhaps typically, these things never happen. These things never happen because we live in a fallen world; how much worse we would have trouble coping with these things were it not for our Lord, who we know loves us. Undercurrent revolves around two people who specifically seek closure from their lives – Kanae from her husband's disappearance; Hori from both his sister's kidnapping and his subsequent emotional detachment from his home – and neither find it. They do the only thing that they can do, which is move forward. While there is a good time to pause and mourn, to cease life's activities to help accept the pain and despair that come with such losses, certainly Christ would not direct us to stand still indefinitely as opposed to draw near to Him, and certainly the Holy Spirit would not protect our innermost being merely for us to be static. In fact, it is only because of our knowledge of Christ and His love for us that we continue on past these tragedies, as Kanae and Hori do, regardless of how we feel, in spite of who we are.

First, follow Kanae's essential conflict, her husband being absent without reason. At the beginning of the work, the husband has only been gone a little while, but by the end, it has been roughly a year. The husband's disappearance both takes up a lot of Kanae's thoughts and yet is something she is concerned little with, as it weighs too heavily upon her for her to lift it on her own. If not for the appearance of Kanae's friend and the subsequent introduction of the detective Yamazaki, Kanae likely would have only vaguely dwelt upon it, being completely consumed with trying to run the Tsuki no Yu, having a depression settle upon her whenever she sees a news story about a death or disappearance and forever being confused about what would have prompted his disappearance. Up until the final chapter, the sense of Kanae's husband is of abject confusion, even as bits and pieces about his life which were not previously known to Kanae are found out. She starts out the work indirectly setting her mind on him; whenever Hori first comes to help with the bathhouse, she sees a newspaper article about a suicide and asks, "What distinguishes the suicides that make it into the paper from those that don't? . . . It's not like everyone who commits suicide is in the news, right?"[1] She is thinking on the absence of any sense of her husband – no reports, no sightings, and most importantly and most distressing is no communication from him directly, the person she loved and married. Even before this, news reels on unidentified bodies and the like capture her attention, and she reads her husband into them, hoping to find something of an end to the haunting within them. The outward grief she has at the time of his disappearance fades into a dulling and ubiquitous sensation that seems to define her life and work. However, as bits and pieces of her husband are revealed by Yamazaki's detective work, still no conclusion can really be made from the truth. Yamazaki finds that Kanae's husband seems to be alive and deliberately left – emptying his bank account the day of his disappearance to do so – and that he seems to have lied about many things, such as being an orphan and the reasons he was fired from his last job. However, it's been too long to locate the husband in person. In all, it seems like the case is a bust. Kanae's reaction throughout has been shock and disbelief, but at Yamazaki's conclusion that the husband is alive and untraceable: "No, this is enough. It seems like he has no intention of returning after all… And that may have been what it was all about. . . Perhaps this's because I feel like I didn't grasp a part of who he really was..."[2] She blames herself for the situation, but accepts the mistake and moves on – that is, until her husband meets with Yamazaki and says he wants to meet Kanae again. Kanae agrees, and of course, there is even in this no final closure even her husband can provide: "I'm a liar. . . no matter what, I couldn't do it [confess the truth to Kanae]. No matter how many times I tried to tell you, Whenever [sic] it got close to the truth, I got scared and couldn't say anything."[3] There was no ulterior motive. He simply liked lying and couldn't stop lying, and when his lies started to catch up to him, he ran. Kanae is not the first and seems likely to not be the last. Instead of fury, she addresses her husband tenderly for this last time: "Don't catch a cold and don't skip your meals. . . When you send the divorce papers, make sure you write your address. . . It would've been good for us if we'd been able to talk like this before."[4] Kanae's peace is simply because she knows she can move on; she has seen her husband again, and that is going to have to be enough. It would've been good if they were able to talk like such before, but they were not able and it is over now. As she said before, it is far too late now, and life has to go on without him and without having any reason or sense to attach to her husband’s disappearance. She steels herself for the life ahead, a life she has already been living but refused to accept before this point. She had wandered to the left and to the right of her living, even if she seemed to keep well with her necessary tasks. Once the pathway has become straight for her, it is not so difficult for her to travel down it, to not merely do things but to be a human being who is alive.

Lastly, follow Hori's story. Hori remembers his sister's kidnapping many years ago, and though he doesn't realize Kanae was actually present for that event, he remembers how close they were, so when he sees that the bathhouse Kanae's family owned is in need of help, he asks his union if he can be sent there. His appearance throughout the work is ominous but never threatening. We establish him as an ultimate force of good, at least from Kanae's perspective, when he intervenes with the perverted child, how he consoles the child and also steps in for him, rwturning the underwear the kid stole and protecting his identity - but Hori also keeps a sense of obscurity about his own actions this way. His connection to Kanae is not revealed until the very end of the manga. Hori's presence is generally unattached and dissociated. He says little, and swats away any serious conversation, especially from Kanae:

"...Hori-san?"
"Yes?
"–
". . .is this about what you asked me in the car? . . ."
"If it were about what I asked you in the car, would you be able to answer? . . ."
"Ma'am?"
"Yes?"
"Good night."[5]

There are many cases like this. Despite that connection to her he feels because of his sister, nothing could ever come of it, no closure could be found in Kanae, no difference in neither the way things were before nor the way things will be after could be made. They can't relate. Even when Hori realizes Kanae also holds the pain of his sister in her, he still can't see any way to reach out to her in a meaningful way, and it is likely that there truly is no way for the two to connect. Though Sabu-jii, a regular of the Tsuki no Yu, tries to talk Hori up, "If you get yourself involved in her personal affairs, you'll be more than just two individuals! You should deal with your suffering together," Hori's conclusion is very bitter: "...I don't think there's anything I can do for Kanae-san. I shouldn't have come here."[6] This is why he acts the way he acts throughout the work, for he knows from the beginning that his quest for closure is doomed, that he will not find anything even vaguely resembling some kind of closure, and he hates that. He wants to find his place and find his home after his family moved away and shattered. He wants to stop blaming himself for the kidnapping of his sister, but he quite can't. He has to live with reality. Ultimately this plays into the confusion of Kanae's life too – if Hori is not present to hurt Kanae nor her business, then what is he there for? - and it also serves as the work's core duality; even before the faux resolution, Kanae has accepted the way things are and has found peace, but Hori cannot find any peace even after his thoughts about his situation are shown to be correct. Hori's bitterness does not stop him from leaving, however. His bitterness is the thing that causes him to move on with his life, while Kanae's peace is what enables her to move on with hers. This is not a showcase of how people need different motivators to do the right thing, but rather the opposite: regardless of if one feels completely at peace with life or is in deep despair about it, one must keep going forward.

The difference between Kanae's and Hori's ultimate attitude towards their circumstance is not a difference of morality; one is not better than the other. Kanae is joyed to see things to their end, and so she moves forward. Hori is distraught to realize things are at their end, and so he moves forward. We are not called to position ourselves just so we can properly follow Christ; rather, Christ meets us where we are and demands that we follow Him from that point forward no matter what else may be present in our lives. The Christian has hope for their end, as we trust our Savior to deliver us into the Kingdom of Heaven, and faith is the substance of that which we have yet to see. Consider the third chapter of the book of Hebrews. [Exodus 17, Numbers 14, Deuteronomy 1, Psalm 95] See that the ultimate call, that which the Christ is praised for, is faithfulness, His faithfulness to us, His house, that as His house we must necessarily partake in the Houseowner, the Carpenter. Consider that the evil heart is the unbelieving heart, that the loathed generation is the one which is looking to the place it was before, the place before the Lord saved them, that it is their unbelief which barred them from entry to the promised land – and consider that even the loathed generation were kept by the Lord because they ultimately followed Him. That is the calling of the Christian, to follow, and to be lead to our final home, the place Christ is preparing for us now. This, the hope of Heaven, is for that which we have yet to see, however, and not our present time, for if we could see it, then hope would have already come to pass and been fulfilled. We must live our lives unresolved. What resolution did those who died in the wilderness have? It would be unwise to suggest that they should all be subject to eternal damnation, as they continued to follow the Lord even after this casting out, so we cannot blame it merely on them being heathens, and Scripture is explicit that, excepting Joshua and Caleb, all of that generation was loathed, so we cannot cite any kind of unmentioned bias. Surely you do not fashion yourself a man as great as Moses or Joshua. Therefore be careful not to assume yourself a leader and find you are taking yourself back into the jaws of Egypt; even if the Lord should not guide you with pillars of cloud and fire, to assume that you are therefore able to lead yourself is to enthrone yourself upon your heart and to throw the Holy Spirit off from you, who knows that with His depart He has prepared your house for demons worse than that which you knew before He had arrived. We all have provoked Him; we all have separated ourselves from Christ; we all have been disobedient; we all have incurred the full wrath of Christ upon ourselves, are solely responsible for all of these things. It is silly to suggest that the support of Christ should promptly bring us out of the wilderness and restore us immediately into the promised land, for not even a man as great as Moses could find this to be true. Rather, we are the loathed generation, and it is precisely the loathing, the absence, the confusion, and the distortion of the world we live in that should prompt us to cultivate a believing heart, to partake in Christ and encourage one another to do the same. Life is not about making it to some good place eventually. Life is about longing for Christ, and about His death and resurrection so that one day our longing may be fulfilled. Let us also remember to trust in the Lord and not ourselves – that is, if we set to do a good thing, and a good thing is done, then we should know that a good thing has been done regardless of how empty or fruitless it seems to us in that moment. Hori does not leave with what he came looking for; rather, he feels shameful for thinking he could find answers or closure, and empty in realizing the way the world moves along. Nonetheless, when Hori has left Tsuki no Yu, his job as a boiler has been completed, that is, his ultimate task of keeping the bathhouse afloat has been accomplished, it is finished.
