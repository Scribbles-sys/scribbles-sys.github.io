The Compass Rose & Direction

Return to catalog

In The Compass Rose, Le Guin collects stories she's written that, in her words, "go off each in its own direction. . . Within it, various circling motions may be perceived. . ."[1] The book is organized by six directions: the four cardinal directions, and nadir and zenith, which are terms used with celestial spheres to indicate directly below and directly above a certain point. The book, in many ways, is an examination of direction and what it means for one’s life. Each of the twenty stories is assigned a direction. "The reasons [for this]. . . are not very serious," Le Guin says in the preface to the book. "Nadir may be down underground, for instance, or in the depths, or simply down-hearted.”"[2] In the writing of these stories and in their organization, there is a focus placed on a character's location, not physically but in a general sense. The Christian may find themselves in want of a compass in trying to navigate life. Our situation in our fallen world is so dire that we usually cannot grasp what direction we are even facing, let alone which way we should be going, especially as the way we want to go is usually the worst choice. Christ has not let us have some kind of sense of direction, but merely tells us to go. This is a common aspect of all of humanity, a symptom of our Imago Dei; we do not know which way we are facing, and while we have a vague sense of where we want to be, in the form of goals, aspirations, et cetera, we don't know in any real sense where that is or how to arrive there. This is what The Compass Rose gives to its reader, vagueness and confusion, as life gives it to us, not bitterly but neutrally. One story from each directional heading will be examined: "The New Atlantis," "Two Delays on the Northern Line," "The First Report of the Shipwrecked Foreigner to the Kadanh of Derb," "The Eye Altering," "The Water is Wide," and "Sur."

"The New Atlantis" is assigned to the direction Nadir. This story is the most interesting in relation to the theme of direction because it is the only one that explicitly, at the forefront, plays with the chosen direction's antithesis, Zenith – this story is about being down, but is constantly reminding its reader that the down will be up one day. The plot is about the wife of a mathematician in an U.S.A. where mathematics and humanities have been banned. The narrator of this story is her own character separate from the world around her, but the world (specifically the government) chains her down. The unemployed are barred from any kind of welfare except through a years-long process – though they must take this welfare if they remain unemployed, women are banned from being doctors, hitchhiking is illegal, marriage is illegal, et cetera. Simon has sidestepped the American system and had published a paper in China, which has put him in very hot water – a few years of jail and continued harassment from the FBI once he is released early from back injury. "He was with me for twelve days," the narrator laments at the end of the story.

"On January 28th the crats came from the Bureau of Health Education and Welfare and said that since he was receiving unemployment compensation while suffering from an untreated illness, the Government must look after him and restore him to health, because health is the unalienable right of the citizens of a democracy. . . The chief Health Officer pulled his gun and said that if he continued to struggle he would shoot him for resisting welfare. . ."[3]

The Nadir of the story is so obvious as to never need comment – the narrator rarely comments on it directly herself. However, fighting against this downward pull is a sideplot of the New Atlantis the story is named after. This city is only mentioned briefly thoughout the plot proper, but it has a great affect on it because it is literally and representative of the fight against the system they live under, the fight against what their world has become. From the beginning, it is shown as a space of hope and life: "It did not occur to us that there might be another moment. There was no reason to assume that there might be more than one. One was marvel enough: that in all that field of the dark, in the cold, heavy, dense, moveless, timeless, placeless, boundless black, there should have occurred, once a small, slightly blurred, moving light!"[4] This city is within and centered around the narrator, but it is not confined to her – notice her use of "us" throughout her descriptions of it, and that other people do bring it up quite often in the plot proper. As the story progresses, the descriptions of Atlantis become more and more clear; at the same time, the world Le Guin has created for the story grows more bleak. The reader is given more context to the kind of government that rules over the land and the society that government creates. No one has any heart in looking towards the future, even when the narrator"s husband and some other scientifically inclined friends of his manage to create a portable source of power not owned by the government. At this climax the story becomes its most explicitly grieved: "I did not much want to listen to them talk tonight. . . Only because it grieved me terribly, their talking. Because they couldn't rejoice aloud over a job done and a discovery made, but had to hide there and whisper about it. Because they couldn't go out into the sun."[5] Yet despite this, and despite there being no conceivable end to this horror, the Atlantis is the most hopeful just following this:

"We are here. When we break through the bright circle into life, the water will break and stream white down the white sides of the towers, and run down the steep streets back into the sea. The water will glitter in dark hair, on the eyelids of dark eyes, and dry to a thin white film of salt.
"We are here."[6]

The Nadir is entrapping – Atlantis never does rise out of the waters, not now – but it is not defining, as the concluding statement made on the city shows. It would be wrong to say the Zenith of the people's gaze is more important or more defining than the Nadir of their reality, but the Zenith is what is ultimate. The Zenith is the end. It seems, and is, so far away, but this is only because it is too close to touch. It is here with us, as much as the Nadir is.

Contrary to the permanent sense of direction found in "The New Atlantis," "Two Delays on the Northern Line" is about not having any sense of direction or agency at all. It is assigned the direction North. This story is composed of two little stories about two Eduards who have had distant family members die and are required to deal with the aftermath – not emotionally, as they both feel nothing, but socially and legally. Eduard Orte, of the beginning story, is dealing with the former. We are introduced to him in great rain and deep blackness. "A two-hour train trip had stretched into an afternoon of shunting, waiting, crawling from one village to another all through the hills of the upper Molsen province in heavy, inexhaustible rain."[7] This opening establishes two themes common throughout the story: (1) that direction is a disassociated fact not truly grasped (they are in the "upper Molsen," a definition based on a map, nothing that is seen or felt, et cetera) and (2) moving in this direction is slow, painful, and defeating. Orte is being dragged away from his job by another severe attack from his mother.

"Their mother. . . had not the faintest desire to 'see' either Eduard or Restia, to have her routine disrupted and her hoarded vitality called upon for an expenditure of specious interest in her doings, which had not interested her for years. But Restia needed the expectable, the conventional, so badly that she regularly employed the inconvenient to achieve it. When wired COME AT ONCE to the sick mother’s bedside, one comes."[8]

Only, this time, the mother has died, and because of the train being delayed, Orte was not there for it. The second story is about Eduard Russe, whose death in his family is even more distant than Orte's: a great-uncle who Russe does not know dies, and his house is willed to him. This interrupts his life much as Orte’s mother was interruptive. As the narrator describes, "His days were punctuated by these dashes, not periods but breaks, empty spaces in which he stopped himself from finishing the thought, or from trying to finish the thought which no longer had an end. . . in any case all thoughts led to this same non-end or stopping place and were there, as in the cremator's fire, destroyed."[9] The plan is obvious: Russe has no connection nor need of this distant house, so he's going to sell it and enjoy himself with all the extra money. This plan changes once he arrives at the house and spends the night there, and so suddenly he feels the need to move there, so suddenly forms that connection he did not have, sees that the house holds hope for his renewal, a vague and distant but real hope, and foresees "his request for a transfer to the Bureau here in Barilava, the wild cherry flowering in the mountains in March, his second marriage, all that, but for the moment enough, this room, the swords, the sunlight; he had arrived."[10] It is relieving but it is also meaningless – a life not unlike his own now, a life yet to exist. The relief is based on the sense of movement, that Russe might be going somewhere and doing something, might be making progress in some form, might experience something different. This is contrasted with Orte's ending, which is not relieving. The final death of his mother, the reunion of his family, none of it matters in any way. Russe’s change does matter, it is something even in its vanity. Both of them arrive somewhere, but Orte's certainty only sends him deeper into confusion, whereas Russe's ascends him from his confusion somewhat.

"The First Report of the Shipwrecked Foreigner to the Kadanh of Derb" is listed under the East section. This direction, from the American perspective, suggests the exotic and the alien, the novel and the unknown. This is true of this story, but it is only supposedly foreign to its imagined audience; the story itself is just describing Venice, the city in Italy. There is a further dichotomy beyond the audience being the unfamiliar Kadanh and the relatively aware reader, as the narrator themselves is not familiar with Venice, only having been there four times for a couple of weeks worth in total. Despite this, the author views Venice as Earth, as home. It is clear that this story is, among other things, about the experience of reading from the perspective of the one being read: "[I]t occurred to me that there may be an alternative motive to your request. In asking me to describe my world, you may not be seeking information about my world at all. You may not plan to listen to my words, but only to the silences between the sentences, from which you will learn a good deal about your own world."[11] The duality of reading is that the most foreign and exotic places, so foreign and exotic that they do not really exist, can be the places that reveal to us the things most real. Le Guin then puts this conundrum onto the narrator and protagonist, who finds home most present in that which among the least known to them among all the things they know, a city they vacationed to a few times. The narrator cannot know Venice because they have barely been there, and yet they do know Venice because Venice is Earth, Venice is home. How much less of the New Heaven and the New Earth do we know of than we do of Venice? How much more foreign a world where we are unable to be separated from Christ is than one that is across an ocean is from us, how much more difficult it is to describe it. How much greater, too, our longing for it, and how much greater our sense that this is home safe. It is somewhere we have not been and can never really be. The narrator ends the story, "Winter and summer, the gondolas were black, They were painted black in mourning for something. . . The warning cry of the gondolier. . . 'Hoy-y-y,' he called, and the cats and the lions on the sun-warmed angles of the bridges said nothing, as you, my lord, do now."[12] The story ends with a resounding sense of loneliness. It is unanswered, it is necessary. It is reading, it is literature. How often does our Lord stay silent to us, and how much more does the Lord love us and ponder over us than we do the great and/or thought provoking novels and other stories we have read.

"The Eye Altering" is put on Zenith. This story is about both being oriented and moving towards the Zenith, but the protagonist, Miriam, can experience only the former. Miriam is a depressing and irritable person. She is the doctor for a Jewish colony on a distant planet. She hates being there and longs for the old world. The bodies of humans cannot tolerate the diet of the world, and so they must take many medicines, primarily a metabolizing pill, in order to be able to survive on the world. Miriam is, like the sun of this new world, "settling, rather than setting, dully down onto the horizon,"[13] for this planet has twenty eight hour days. There are many such discrepancies, and they make most of the colonists, especially Miriam, irritable. One of Miriam’s patients is Genya, a generally sickly man who was born on the planet and is in and out of the infirmary Miriam works at. The two have become genuine friends because of this, but they clash often because Genya, being born on the planet, loves it, and Miriam hates it. She cannot see it as home, until Genya's painting suddenly makes it clear to her when it is hung in their gallery. Among the paintings they have of Earth, the New Zion painting does not stand out but rather looks just like Earth. Once she realizes, Miriam exclaims "How do we know? How do we know what a child of Zion sees? We can see the picture in this light tha's like Home. Take it outside, into the daylight, and you'll see what we always see, the ugly colors, the ugly planet where we’re not at home. But he’s at home! He is!"[14] Most remarkable is how quickly, in light of this revelation, Miriam becomes joyful. It would have been easier for her to have stayed downtrodden and became envious of Genya, or to ignore his revelation because it meant nothing to her, but instead she is glad both on his behalf and for herself, because she has realized that New Zion is not Not Home in an objective sense, but just something she has not been granted access to. She is like Moses looking out upon the land promised by the Lord to the Hebrews, knowing he will never see it but happy knowing that it is real, that the Lord's promises are real. "The child carr[ying] a bunch of the repulsive blackish-purple native weeds" does not morph into "as a child at home would carry a bunch of white daisies, red poppies picked in the fields,"[15] but she can see now that these dark weeds are this girl's daisies and poppies, that the dark weeds are homely and pretty because this planet is their Home. There is comfort to be had in the changing of times, that as it may seem as though the Lord has sent us to a place to be cast away, the truth is that He is preparing a place for His children, a place they truly and intimately know to be Home, and no sense of unbelonging can overpower that.

"The Water is Wide" is the most difficult story to understand. It's plot is fairly simple: a man suffers from illness and madness and his sister breaks him out of the psych ward. After breaking out, we see Gideon's point of view, and the story deteriorates from there: "I am Anna. I am Gideon. I am Gideanna. I am sister's brother, brother's sister. I am Gideon who am dying, but it is your death I die, not mine. I am Anna who am not mad, but I am your brother, who is mad"[16] The story is named after a folk song originally written in 1906, a song which the story also quotes:

"The water is wide, I cannot get over
And neither have I wings to fly
Give me a boat that can carry two
And both shall row, my love and I."[17]

The song in general is about imaging a solution to an impossible task and mourning over the lack of it. It is about living a life that does not provide answers or closings. Neither does this story provide answers or closings, only that Gideon and Anna, Gideanna, do seem to make it over the wide waters finally in death and are reunited with their loved ones in the confusing but blissful afterlife they find themselves in. It is odd that Anna should die, for she seems to have no issues, but she is made to identify with her brother as she visits him. She begins the story somewhat distant and ambivalent, resisting Gideon's cries to be let out, but in a letter to a friend she writes, "[The doctor] is supposed to be the best man here for nervous breakdowns. He has been disapproving lately and says Gideon is deteriorating, ceasing to respond, but all he gives him to respond to is drugs. What is he supposed to say to them?"[18] Subsequently, Anna agrees to Gideon's demands to be broken out of the psych ward. The breakout and breakdown are the climax of the story, but in its climax it becomes muddied and indiscernible. Any point is lost and any direction is let go. This reaching out is painful for the both of them, as they are both made aware of the suffering of the world through the lens of their own infinitesimal existence, that this big step forward into a new life seems as meaningless and indiscernible as the maniacal washing of blackboards that landed Gideon in the psych ward to begin with. There is no resolution and there is not meant. The ending is beautiful. Letting go of the ground is not the story pushing off traditional methods of closure but embracing them in excess. In spite of everything, life is beautiful; even in madness, life is beautiful, as the prose ending this story is beautiful.

Finally, the last story in the book is titled "Sur," and is assigned to the direction of its name. The story is of a group of nine women from South America in 1909 aspiring to go to Antarctica and the South Pole, as expeditions there are becoming popular and the continent is not far away from them relatively speaking (Chlie and Argentina both have claims to and bases on Argentina, of only seven countries in the world to have land claims on the Continent.) There is a senselessness in this story, but it is senselessness through the lens of retrospection, summed up in what the narrator has to say about the South Pole: "This is not a place where people have any business to be. We should have turned back; but since we had worked so hard to get there, it seemed that we should go one, at least for a while."[19] Similarly, when they make it to the Pole: "We discussed leaving some kind of mark, or monument, a snow cairn, a tent pole and flag; but there seemed no particular reason to do so."[20] It is movement taken to the extreme; it is what happens when you focus less on where you’re going and more on going itself; it is when you take more time to work out something than you do to deliberate it. The group of women decide to go South, so they go South, and they keep going South, and they keep going South. A lot of preparation is made for this journey, it certainly would not have succeeded without it, but this preparation work is postdeliberate; this preparation work is itself movement South. The work is honest in that this nonsensical way of living is not synonymous with failure; the narrator is open about their regrets but also expresses fondness for the experience. They hide the story in the atiic so that few ever see, but then it is great that she should choose to write this story at all, choose to make its memory permanent and joyful. The end of the journey seems pointless and the results fruitless, but that doesn't mean the journey itself was necessarily a waste – after all, it is the Pole that was "no place people have any business to be" and not necessarily Antarctica. The narrator also is quite happy with the accomplishment of the ordeal, that they and their friends were capable of what they had done. The most impressive thing of all is that one of the girls, Teresa, twenty year old, was pregnant, and the baby had to be delivered as they were waiting on their return ship. All of the women help out with the delivery, and Teresa decides to name the baby Rosa del Sur. This is the last story in the collection, and in these last moments the story looks back over the rest of the story and asks if the journey or the destination was meaningful or should have been done. It is anxious over the directions of all of the other stories, those directions that all come from and relate to the Rose, the unspeakable direction that defines all the others, that all the other directions depend on. It is not a resolution. It is just a fact. It is not happy, joyful, spiteful nor bitter. It just is.

These stories show the varied, impactful, and hectic nature of our lives. They all end, and they all end up somewhere, but none of them give any true resolution; the closest thing anyone receives is a strong notion of the way they should orient themselves. The easy thing to say is that the direction we all long to go is Home, Heaven. This is a true statement, but its application here is cruel, for to suggest it upon our daily musings is to suggest that there is a Heaven to be found here; more than that, it implies that the Lord "wants us home" so to speak. Lest you want to imply that one can just turn towards Heaven and walk inside, or that the reason why the Lord made us is so we can die and nothing else? No, but the Lord created us so that we may wander about, until we are to be picked back up again. The directions in these stories do suggest themselves to the Christian life in many ways, but another essay of similar or longer length would be required to detail them all. The reader can see these for themselves, can follow them where the Holy Spirit should guide them to go. This is the way that the Lord has implemented things, not just for our intellectual pursuits, but for every moment of our lives. Consider the missionary work of the Apostle Paul. [Acts 16] Only once did he ever receive direct divine intervention, and this was only because of Paul’s insistence in doing what the Lord had been holding him back from. Otherwise, Paul simply went where he fancied, where he thought that he ought to go. This is what The Christ created and called Paul to do. This is the epitome of the Christian life. One needs discipline and discernment, but one cannot discipline themselves into heirship, nor can one discern Christ from that which opposes Him; only the Holy Spirit can make one do this and, as the Lord told Moses in the wilderness when Moses asked what to say to the Hebrews, [Exodus 3], "I will be present where I choose to be present." We then are called to live, and whether or not we are prone to or decide to worry or fret over where Christ is taking us is not important, because Christ has told us to go and we must obey.
