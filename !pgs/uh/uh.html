Universal Harvester & Familiarity

Return to catalog

In speaking of remedies against the fierceness and rage in the madness of natural corruption, Edward Reynolds writes:

"Fifthly and lastly, in the ministry of the Word, when thy bosom-sin is met with, and the plague of thine own heart discovered; when thou art pricked in thy master-vein; when the edge of the sword enters to the quick, sacrificeth thee, crucifieth thy lusts, cuts off thy earthly members, ransacks thy conscience, and shews thee the inside of thy foul soul; here by all means look unto thy heart; never so likely a time for madness and fierce opposition to set up itself, as when a man is driven into a corner and cannot fly."[1]

Here Reynolds warns of our want to turn away from the darkness in our hearts as opposed to confronting it. We are aware it is there, and we know it demands our presence, but we ignore it because to tend to it is painful and burdensome, requiring much work and patience and humility, things which we are want to spare with all of the other things we have going on in our outer lives. To avoid it is unacceptable, however, because such darkness will overtake us if it is not tended to, specifically in our weakness from drudgery. We would rather continue living life the way we are, patching issues in ourselves only when critical failure arises and continuing on unabated by any warning signs or the common sense of our own natures. This position is the one we find the characters of Universal Harvester in. Jeremy, whom is one of the three focal points of the book, is living with his father and making some kind of living at the slowly fading Video Hut, a VHS rental store managed by Sarah Jane, the second focus. Life continues on this monotonous path for all of the residents of Nevada, Iowa, until someone starts cutting the tapes and stitching dreadful but not harmful home movies onto some of the rental tapes – scenes such as blindfolded people standing on one leg, blanketed masses being forcefully kicked. Jeremy is very slow and even ultimately reluctant to investigate because he fears anything that might threaten his current life – homegrown mysteries, his dad finding finally finding another woman to date, Jeremy finding a proper career, et cetera. Only an investigation of his heart finally prompts these changes, and primarily this comes from two places: finding his coworker Ezra passed out in a wreck on the side of a road, and participating in the making of those movies once he finally starts digging into the phenomenon. The movies are, in fact, catharsis for Lisa Sample, the third focus, a woman who never found her mother, and they serve as catharsis for the participants in many ways, too. Universal Harvester also shows the contradiction between our aversion to our innermost selves and our desperate yearning for the familiar, as the obsession with the status quo, among other things, illustrates.

The home movies themselves remain ambiguous but are addressed and brought up much throughout the novel. They are not random or merely meant to scare, neither. As the novel itself states, "a narrative begins to emerge that's hard to convey in general terms, but I am reminded of it when I watch the third scene on the tape marked Shed #4." On this tape, people with their heads covered and vision obscured wander around. "No one seems to be minding the camera; after a while one of the guests runs into the tripod, and the two fall together to the dirt floor. The camera is then trained by chance on the hooded face. . . the others run into the fallen figure, but hold themselves upright, recalculating their rough parabolas, trying to make sense of the new data."[2] There are too many scenes to rigorously catalog them all here, but this is the best direct representation of the home videos spliced onto the tapes. There are people and they cannot see and they wander the best that they can, and when "new data" emerges they try to cope with it but cannot, ultimately, because the removal of the familiar has completely altered them and how they see the world. They have come to expect life to be a certain way and have donned head coverings, for what use is sight when you have seen everything? They have robbed themselves, perhaps most charitably have had the sense instilled into them by someone who came before them, of exploration and discovery and knowing in any meaningful sense. The blinding of their eyes could only have been initiated with a heart that had forsaken the sight it had been granted. There is much more going on here: the attempt to acclimate one's self to people one can never understand deep enough to know what even the next expected move is; the inevitability of the camera being hit with such circumstances, as the Lord inevitably reveals what we have tried to put into darkness, completely disorienting if not outright destroying anyone caught in the wake of the revelation; the failure of human thought to capture the obvious and certain in a practical way – how obvious to the others roaming around that someone had fallen over, how apparent the solutions that could be taken, and yet there is absolutely nothing they could do; the tendency to define life only by its revelations, to try and bury every day we have been given alive and suffocate it that it comes back no more, waiting patiently only for the strike, for the wrath, for the new data; et cetera. The situation is disturbing and tense. It is to see the depth of the human condition on display. Another scene is equated by Sarah Jane to a kind of final scene, even though none of the home movies truly suggest any sense of continuity: "[A] woman, fleeing, had only rough moonlight to guide her… where? Across the highway? Into the road? She ran, canvas hood in hand, growing smaller as she made progress, the sound of her footfalls fading into silence."[3] An escape, but not a final one: not into light and the heavens, but neither unto a burning abyss, but into the blackness, the silence, the unknown and unpresent. This is the escape into life, life on Earth, one that is distant from the Holy Spirit but embiodied by Him nonetheless, life which awaits the characters and which the confrontation of our inner beings necessitates. Sarah Jane, the one who views this scene as the final one, sees it as "The last blurred burst of information before the transmissions stopped coming forever."[4] Most striking in the novel are those scenes which the trio themselves eventually participate in:

"Lisa's voice, offscreen, sounds suddenly warmer now; it's hard to account for it. 'How did you recognize my driveway?' she says.
"'From the movies,' says Jeremy."[5]

Though we aren't shown the scene in the book, there is talk of Jeremy being hit and bruised and bloodied through the runtime of the video. They carry the most impact for the reader since they are among the only scenes centered on a character we are familiar with ourselves, and they are the most personal since the scenes involve some of the only dialogue we see from the videos and the deepest interactions. Consider also: "'Look, I do it for myself, too, I know that,' says Lisa, casting her gaze out now past the tripod, around it and out to the tall rows of corn on the neighboring property, the pigs in the distance." Lisa is with "the friend she made of the lonely woman from Nevada. . .,"[6] with Sarah Jane. It is the opening up these people have shared that has facilitated this limited connection; mostly, this experience has been about understanding who they are as individuals.

Very important to the effect these tapes have on this trio specifically is the state that they are all in when the doctored tapes begin to show themselves. Jeremy, at the start of a novel, is at the tip of but not yet having entered transition. He is ready and capable to care for himself in the world he inhabits, willing and able to make something of himself beyond money to get by, though we never see a glimpse of whatever it is he seeks to obtain. He is also coping with the death of his mother with his father. She died in a car wreck six years ago, so the pain has receded now, but the grief remains, mostly because the two of them have not journeyed through it, have remained within that life. The most poignant passage to illustrate this is this one: "He didn't resolve anything yet, but he registered the presence of some slow movement inside himself, a small change in the coordinates of his inner drift. . . The last thing he saw before he fell asleep was the glint of a baseball trophy he'd gotten in fourth grade. . . It was a statue of a player with a bat in his hands, waiting for the pitch."[7] This is not a complete picture, of someone waiting for life to hand them something; Jeremy cannot seem to see all of the balls life is pitching to him already, for he has his head turned aside, scared of the speed or the curve, scared of failure or of what success might mean for him, or something else. This sense of transition is important because it marks the entire novel; it is as though everyone were on the top of a tall hill, not willing to move anywhere unless prompted by something to look towards the horizon and finally be convinced that something would wait for them down there. Everyone has this sense that there is more to the world than what they know but would rather cling to that which they have already experienced. This becomes painfully apparent when unidentifiable home movies begin to be spliced into the tapes at Jeremy's work. They are disturbing, but not necessarily violent; only two times in the novel do we ever come across anyone being hurt in any way on the tapes, both of which have been mentioned in this essay. Everyone is curious to know where the tapes com from and what they really are. One of the customers who watched the spliced tapes begins to form her own theories in a journal; Sarah Jane begins to search for and later work with the tape splicer. Jeremy does nothing. He feels fine where he is. He loves his life because his life is familiar. The tapes provide a sense of dread not because they imply snuff or anything silly like this but because of the sheer rawness of them. They are videos of unadulterated people thrown into unfamiliar situations and forced to be themselves. Jeremy is not oblivious to the effects of the videos. This effect is the reason he detests them. He is still waiting for the pitch, even if he is never swinging, but the videos do manage to leave their mark anyway; eventually Jeremy does punt a job offer Bill Veatch gives him to start working in the state capital. It brings Jeremy to his senses. "But this isn't Hollywood. It's Des Moines. Jeremy didn't feel fear when he thought about life at Veatch & Son. He felt – what was the word?– inspired." This is a great improvement, but still, "He could take the job with Bill and quit Video Hut, or he could turn down the job and stay where he was."[8] The choice has been made by this point, of course – even here, Jeremy unconsciously decides not to go straight home but go tell Sarah Jane that he is going to quit. Still present is his yearning for the comforts of his own routine, the things he knows, but the encounter with things he doesn't know fills him with a sense of purpose. He realizes all of the sudden that he is a complex human being and that he ought to start acting like one. A customer comes into the store after his decision, and "The whole conversation felt like it belonged to another era, a time of reliable coefficients, and his mind was on Collins now, on trying, before he moved on, to get some glimpse of what had disturbed Video Hut's once-inviolate stillness, its perennial motionless static present, a thing already passing into legend."[9] Jeremy prepares himself, still unconsciously to him but ultimately still, that seeking of his heart. He has sought out his outer conditions, explored his familiarity, and mapped his territory, but this is great in its inadequacy. Jeremy most come to know his inner self and face not merely grief and agony but him, face Jeremy as a complex human being and everything that entails. It is a haunting experience, raw just like every other video was. The person who was with him on the way to Collins, the woman with the journal who persuades him to go, wants to call the police after it is all better, but Jeremy knows better, having been there. When someone stumbles upon those tapes years after that, and messages Jeremy about them, Jeremy's ultimate response: "'You should leave her alone,' is exactly what he wrote. 'You probably won't. I know how it is. I've seen it personally. But I wish you would. . . Don't take this personally but all of this stuff is none of your business. Sincerely, Jeremy Heldt.'"[10] Jeremy has come into his own, has found wisdom and calm and discovered life for what it is, but this only came about because he allowed himself to be painfully introspective, subjected himself to the innermost workings of his being, allowed them to be found out. If he had kept everything hidden, had kept his inner man at rest and given him no materials, had clung only to what was familiar to him, he would have never managed to make a life for himself, whatever kind of life that may be now. There is also, of great importance, Sarah Jane. As opposed to Jeremy's "This is something we should just leave alone," Sarah Jane says "I have to go back." Even as she begins "straightening up the front counter a little: just keeping her hands busy, easing unsurely back into the normal quiet of the day," she is too changed to let go and repeats, "I have to go back."[11] She is enamored by the videos, by the sense of the unfamiliar. Instead of taking shelter in what she knows, Sarah Jane comes to be assailed by it. There is a twisted logic, though, in that she too pushes off the videos until she realizes the videos are not random found footage but deliberate acts of sabotage, not a prank but a deliberate affect by a local. These videos contain landmarks Sarah Jane recognizes; she is the one who relays this information to Jeremy, that all of the videos have been shot near their town of Nevada. That something so strange and affronting could really be something she knows is too much for Sarah Jane to bear, and she must be involved:

"You'd expect, in the presence of any inner struggle, to see it reflected here, in the privacy of the highway, where no one is watching. But that after-midnight conversation [of distributing Lisa's doctored tapes onto the shelves of Video Hut], and any news it brought about the nature of Lisa's work, has gone away to hide in the place where all the other moments they share end up: in a secret chamber of Sarah Jane's heart, where the person she'd hoped to be by now has set up shop and is making do with the available materials."[12]

This familiarity fiercely ignited in Sarah Jane what Jeremy at that point is only beginning to accept and come around to: the complexities of the person, the totality of their wants and desires and values and goals, the sum of their dreams and nightmares, fears and hopes. She is coming into her full self, but this has required a deep parsing of who she is, and like Jeremy, it is not a particularly pretty sight – not affronting in the way direct violence is, but eschewing that manner of classification to begin with, being its own separate thing. The darkness within her had produced a null and sulking affect. It had dulled her into being the distant store manager and nothing more than that. It is only once these tapes come to the store and that Sarah Jane finally views them that the reader sees her come alive, be a person, have emotions and stutter around and struggle immensely to accomplish simple tasks like explaining the revelation of the videos being filmed nearby to Jeremy on account of all of the prior things. This parsing of the heart is needed to awaken her. Finally, of course, there is Lisa, the one doctoring the tapes, the one who is the most affected by her inner workings, and in the absence of a mother, Irene, to cling to, and unable to cope together with her father, who is too obsessed with finding Irene to pay much attention towards his daughter, Lisa inherits that obsession and turns it into her art form. A quote from her backstory speaks for itself, among all else said of her thus far:

"[A] child [never] forgets the time she shared in the backyard with her mother gardening, or the fun they had together watching Bedknobs and Broomsticks at the Astro. It just means she learns to bear it. . . It's efficient, and flexible. . . But the rest of the details about it aren't observable from the outside. You have to be closer than you really want to get to see how it works."[13]

These people understand, intrinsically, that they are not good people – not that they're awful people, or that they're malignant forces within their own world, but that they are not good people. They are disturbed people. They are broken people. They are worn-down people. It's not about how much they hurt other people or abuse their gifts or destroy the environment. These are not thoughts in their minds. Perhaps even Reynolds somewhat, in his quoted work, assumes action only, as he pins sin on true and disturbing things; the truth is that sin is not a simple, purposed framework of operation. Sin is not merely one's self facing away from Christ; Sin is the totality of absence from Christ, not merely his absence from our lives after his Ascension to allow the world to drift further away from Him, nor the painful silence we are given in times of persecution or despair, but the complete and total separation from Christ, leaving not one thread open to Him. Consider Isaiah 59. This chapter lays the foundation for sin as acts, iniquities: violence without cause, speech without reason, labor without fruit but instead destruction, the thoughts in their heads themselves evilness, wickedness embedded in even the steps on the roads they travel upon. This is mere foundation, however, and the speech does not cease its exploration of sin with them; sin reveals itself truly as justice departs, as righteousness departs, as wisdom departs, as heartwarm departs; their mere existence could only be speculation to our hearts, as they cannot exist within us. It is from these things that sin multiplies, that iniquity can be enacted. They are the vines that our rebellion grows from. Our bodies are made such that they depart any good thing from within us, and embody us with disturbing, affronting spirits, claiming heirship to wrath alone. The works of the flesh are the evidence of this core within ourselves. There were once, many years ago, mere humans who could intercede to the Lord on the behalf of humanity in their limited capacity, but they were not enough and they were upon the Earth only briefly. Christ Himself had to be sent to save us, for no person could ever do it; no person could ever truly be God on his own, for the same reasons man needed the Lord in the first place. This salvation will never occur to us if we never reckon with that which is already within ourselves. We are sick to know what truly lies within the heart, our ambitions and anxieties and desires and lusts, et cetera. We cannot lie. Our salvation is dependent on us acknowledging this. Let us not forget that withing us lies a heart of darkness, a foaming and incoherent mess, and it is this core with which we strike out against our Creator, against ourselves, against our loved ones, against our homes, against our community, against our environment, against our world, defiling everything we do within every action we take.
