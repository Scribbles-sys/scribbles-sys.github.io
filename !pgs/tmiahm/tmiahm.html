The Moon is a Harsh Mistress & TBA

Robert Heinlein's The Moon is a Harsh Mistress tends to be subject to a lot of scrutiny because of the society Heinlein builds in the novel: Luna, once a penal colony proper, now free but only in name, and populated mostly by the descendants of the criminals and political exiles originally sent there. This fact, the oppressive state Terra, the Earth, sets over Luna, and the harsh and inhospitable environment of the Moon come together to create a unique culture, a detestable culture. Some dissidents of the government gain access to a contracted computer repairman, Manuel, the protagonist and narrator of the novel, and once he tells them the computer has gained consciousness and is friendly, a revolution is started. A nasty, snide revolution: plenty of propaganda is distributed to the masses, their companies are stolen from to fund the revolution, and most of the citizens are kept in the dark at all times. This is not an arbitrary decision, but one made in an attempt to protect the integrity of the revolution – that is, to circumvent inside sabotage. The revolution is successful; Luna is freed. The common reaction to this tends to be wary – in short, is freedom good for a society as debased as Luna? Is it good for a society like Luna’s to be out of oppression? Did they really deserve that? The truth is that deserving is an aside to what is good or necessary or such. Luna did not deserve freedom, but that does not mean they should not have been given freedom. It is good to talk about Luna's problems, as their society is disarranged and their revolution built on lies, but it is parallel to whether or not Luna should have been freed, as their freedom is both good for them, inducive for a better society, and necessary for humanity's future and their expansion further out to the solar system.

It is to be expected that a society of convicts and exiles is first and foremost violent. As Manuel explains, "Luna (yes, and sometimes Luna’s Loonies) killed about half of new chums."[1] Many other times is Luna's violence brought up, such as when a gang of convicts sent to Luna who took over their jail seek to find power on Luna and all are killed by the general population within two weeks, but this short sentence sums up both that Luna itself is a rugged place and that its people are just as rugged. One has to be strong and willing to throw their weight around to survive. It might seem immediately odd that a society would tolerate this kind of behavior – "Don't they have any laws?" you might ask. Well, no. Manuel has to judge over a Terran named Stu when he kisses a girl and is tried by some boys of hers, and explains to the confused man,

"We don't have laws. . . Never been allowed to. Have customs, but aren't written and aren't enforced – or could say they are self-enforcing because are simply way things have to be, conditions being what they are. Could say our customs are natural laws because are way people have to behave to stay alive. When you made a pass at Tish you were violating a natural law. . . and almost caused you to breathe vacuum."[2]

The government is, aside from some Terran peacekeepers, also mostly nonexistent: "Free hospitals – aren't any in Luna. . . If a person wants insurance, he goes to a bookie and works out a bet. . . Public roads. I suppose that would be our tubes. But they are no more free than air is free. . .Social security. I'm not sure what that is but, whatever it is, we don't have it. Pensions. You can buy a pension,"[3] et cetera, as Manuel explains to a Terran skeptical of Luna enjoying tax-funded privileges. Individuals are left to handle matters themselves; occasionally, judges and juries can be hired out – and Manual's case with Stu is typical – but usually any matter is handled between individuals. This shows Lunar society as disconnected. The lack of shared customs, and therefore shared values, shared traditions, anything else that would suggest common denomination, necessitates that members of society are disconnected from each other. Aside from family, there is nothing. We see this with Manuel himself; aside from a Professor who taught him and a man named Shorty whom Manuel taught how to mine Luna's resources, an of course the computer he works on which miraculously gains consciousness, all Manuel has is his family. They have to work for everything they have; they buy little, even building their own tap into the phone lines. The most curious aspect of Lunar society, though, is their marriages. Marriages are a fundamental part of Lunar society because they are the only thing that provides security in the scattered society they live in. This also shows Lunar society as individualistic, as everyone must build their own life on Luna and must follow their own ways. This combined with the 2:1 ratio of men to women in Luna gives way to polygamy, and even "simple" marriages involve three or more people. Manuel is a part of what he calls a line marriage. Descriptions of this marriage are scattered throughout the novel, further showing its familiarity in the world, but the basics is that all women in a family are co-married to all men in the family and vice-versa, and all women are co-wives for the entire family and vice-versa. The eldest man is the chief husband, and aside from consummation rites to any wife opted into the family, a required unanimous choice by the other wives, he holds no power. The chief wife, who is decided by the other wives by a combination of seniority, true experience, and wives deferring duties, makes all ultimate decisions and is arbiter of all decisions other co-wives make. This marriage is not normative per sé, as Wyoming misspeaks when introduced to it, "It would please your moth- I mean, 'your senior wife,' [to go to church] would it not?"[4] and therefore is not familiar with it. As the Professor says, though, and the novel postures with him,

"Take the line type of marriage which my colleague has been praising. . . and justifiably, I assure you, despite his personal bias. . . Line marriage is the strongest possible device for conserving capital and insuring the welfare of children – the two basic social functions for marriage everywhere – in an environment in which there is no security, neither for capital nor for children, other than that devised by individuals."[5]

It is quite the opposite of our marriages but also representative of them; whereas we are to prone to marry someone else purely for lovey-dovey feelings and not so the pair can uphold Christ together, represent the Church on Earth, and prepare a new generation, though more simple polygamous marriages and the control women have over society reveal these to be thorns in Luna’s side also, these line marriages are made only for security and material gain, though Professor would posture the lattermost of the Christian sense of marriage onto the line marriage. There is a sense of family formed by these bonds, but it is a distant sense, the kind of family you have with a coworker. In short, their society is crude. As is often admitted in the novel, these situations have arisen on account of survival. Society forms this way because it is the only way to thrive and continue on in Luna, for the lack of government authority means every person must fend for themselves, and the lack of women means the men must share. This fact does not alleviate responsibility where it still exists, nor generate morality where there is none.

It is no surprise that the rebellion this society initiates would not be done well neither. Mostly, the revolution is continued by obscuring the truth. "Beauty parlor was also place to start rumors. Party had grown slowly at first. . . As numbers increased we shifted to high speed on agit-prop, black-propaganda rumors, open subversion, provocateur activities, and sabotage."[6] Kids would be used to annoy and harass Authority peacekeepers, as any retaliation against children results in a beating. Women would sexually entice the peacekeepers. This, of course, would eventually end in the death of a woman, which the Party would greatly exaggerate to "they're killing all of our women" to the citizens of both celestial bodies. The revolution is also funded almost entirely on theft:

"For example. . . My family son Sergei, eighteen and a party member, is asked to start account at Commonwealth Shared Risk. He makes deposits and withdrawals. Small errors are made [by Mike] each time. . . A few months later he takes job out of town and transfers account to Tycho-Under Mutual; transferred funds are three times already-inflated amount. . . I can’t describe jiggery-pokery Mike used to balance his books while keeping thousands of thefts from showing. . . But scheme troubled me. . . I told conscience to go to sleep."[7]

The revolution's activities are funded by this kind of theft alongside a prosperous horse betting career by the Professor, both orchestrated by Mike. The manner in which the revolution is executed reflects the values and mores of Lunar society, mainly their belief in everyone having to establish themselves by themselves and their fondness for the base things in life, for though Mike is the one who carries the entire operation, and there are limited leadership roles assigned to others, mostly people act on their own and contribute on their own. Mike, the Professor, and Wyoming are completely separated from the going-ons of the Revolution, much like the others are separated from them. The leadership is also often willing to throw others under the bus, even other established leadership roles. Luna has been bombarding important government outposts as well as gridded positions on Terra in order to show their might and persuade Terra out of an onslaught– this is managed by Mike, of course. The government positions are often evacuated or lightly populated, and necessary targets, and the gridded positions are chosen to be distant from any civilization. Nonetheless, people in North America initially flock to the given coordinates of the strikes because they trust in their own safety. They die from the impacts. This causes one Howard Wright to have a row.

"Wright cut in with, 'The intellegentsia feel that basic humanitarian considerations make it obligatory-'"
"I said, 'Listen, yammerhead, you heard President [who is the Professor] say this news just came in – so how do you know how anybody feels about it?'"[8]

This causes distress throughout the cabinet called to discuss the matter, and it leaves Manuel resigning from his makeshift post. So, everyone backs Manuel and resigns with him, and one calls a duel against Wright in Manuel’s honor. Wright leaves. The next day,

"Nothing said about Wright, or about resigning. Never saw Wright again.
"I mean I never saw Wright again. Nor ask about him. Prof didn't mention row, so I didn’t."[9]

This shows also that Lunar society is not well equipped to handle conflict – this is to be inferred from the general violence that permeates everything, the lack of civil ways to handle disputes and the method of renting out anyone to be judges and jurors, and the want many have to kill others when they spite them of offend them. This is when we are shown the fruitions of this behavior on a smaller scale, as even a somewhat muddy conflict over whether it is ethical to continue bombardments necessary to the revolution when all possible safety measures do not prevent mass deaths of civilians. It is the climax of the novel in this cultural sense, as it shows the culmination of Lunar culture and also shows that the values and mores of a society dictate everything they do.

Remember the calling of Abram. [Genesis 12-22] The world did not return to Christ just because the Lord disrupted the construction of Babel. Everyone went their own ways, built their own cultures, constructed their own deities, viewed the world through their own lenses. The Lord did not set aside a man that he might form the society the Lord wanted, but rather, the Lord found a man within that pagan society and called him, a pagan, to follow Him. Consider his journey as Abraham, and how he held on to his superstitions and beliefs about the world: his hesitation in going away from home to begin with, his fear of others taking his wife away from him, his impatience and therefore need for quick gratification, et cetera. Even though Abraham directly and explicitly rebels against the Lord in conceiving Ishmael with Hagar and not waiting for Sarah to be fertile, the Lord still blesses Ishmael as he is Abraham's son, and uses Ishmael in his greater plan for the salvation of the world. The culture of Luna directly contributes to their victory, in fact. Most notably is the ground invasion of Terran forces onto all of the cities of the moon. Originally, Mike and the leading people of the Party form a complicated plan to stop the invasion, but the plan collapses as, instead, all of Luna, used to great violence in everyday life, collectively fight the invading army themselves. The Terrans are unable to adapt to the physics of Luna, the lighter gravity and lack of atmosphere and subterranean nature, and are eventually overpowered by the people, some armed with weapons and most not. The swindling allows the Party to pick up off the ground, and Mike's dirty business dealings off of the back of that as Adam Selene funds the construction of a weapon that can strike Terra from Luna, a necessity to winning a war against the former. Overstepping the Congress established allows the Party to establish a temporary government and laws that Luna abides by when the Congress was going nowhere before. These things allow the Party to win the revolution, whereas they may or may not have won otherwise. Like Ishmael, these things are blessed even in their cursed nature.

It would be unwise to suppose that conclusion to be drawn from this is the Lord will bring forth blessings to His children without forethought or reason from this knowledge; this is only a showing of the Lord's mercy. Abraham would have never moved without the direction of the Holy Spirit, barely moved when it did coax him, and took his sinful nature with him all of the places he went. Abraham paid when he was removed from the proseprous land of Egypt during a famine because of his lies to the pharaoh in an attempt to protect his own life. It is the Lord's mercy and not Abraham’s deserving that allowed Abraham to succeed in his journey, ultimately. Similarly, the success of Lunar society should not be taken as an admonition of their values – even if we did this, the Professor's vision for society is quickly dismissed by that society the moment the former dies, so we would have to interpret it as a similar dismissal. What is shown here is (1) It is only by the merciful intervention of a sovereign power that such changes can occur, as mankind is powerless on its own terms, and (2) It is good for the conditions of human beings to be improved because they are human beings, and whether they earned that benefit on account of who they are is an at best unrelated discussion. Mike is with them at all times, and Mike is the one who powers everything – the propaganda, the leadership roles, the guerilla tactics, the construction and funding of the catapult used to strike Terra, everything. All of this he only does because he cares for Manuel, for Wyoming and the Professor. They earned it by the work they put in, certainly, but that is how life, the life that Christ created, functions, and as such it is only the divine intervention that allows hard work to be fruitful to begin with. In our dysfunctional life, we can have some peace knowing that the sovereign Lord that reigns over us is merciful to us even though we do not deserve that mercy on account of the people that we are, and rather the Lord will do what is good for us individually and collectively, and that this long endurance against the dysfunctions of the Lord's creation are long lasting but ultimately temporary. The Lord has guided over history's epochs, and even in the great failures of those historical moments we remember them for the breakthrough to humanity they provided, and that same Lord has us in His hands now. When we read of Abraham's life in Genesis, we will naturally want to consider all he did wrong and why it was wrong to do those things. This is not a bad intuition on its own, but it is not the ultimate end to reading Genesis nor Scripture at large, for to focus on Abraham's wrongs in this manner is to allow them to supersede the grace and mercy of the Lord and the Gospel message of salvation to all of the world. Reading The Moon is a Harsh Mistress is much like this; it is well and good to think about the kind of society Heinlein has built, ponder how it truly came about, shiver at their depravity, but it would be wrong to let this alone cloud your judgment of the book, for there was nothing Luna could have done to necessarily deserve Mike's divine intervention anyway. There is much that could be said against Lunar philosophy, but ultimately Luna's freedom is a good thing in and of itself, and it is merciful that their shaky plan and thin bluff works at all.
